Daily Reading. Saturday, August fifteenth, twenty twenty-six.

Good morning. Here is the single most useful thing you will hear today, and it is an accounting detail rather than a headline.

A large share of the second-quarter margin beats that the financial press has been describing as operating leverage are not operating leverage at all. They are tariff refunds, and they are landing in cost of goods sold. Stanley Black and Decker booked one hundred eighteen million dollars of pre-tax gain inside cost of sales. Tapestry recognized ninety-six point two million dollars of a probable one hundred fourteen point seven million dollar refund, also against cost of goods. Arhaus had thirty-seven point eight million dollars in cash, plus one point three million in interest, in hand by August sixth. Energizer took sixty-four point one million across nine months.

Why this is the lead item and not a footnote: a refund booked in cost of goods sold inflates gross margin and it inflates EBITDA, in exactly the line a diligence model treats as operational. It is non-recurring. It will not repeat next year. And it will sit inside trailing twelve-month earnings for a full four quarters, quietly distorting every comparable transaction and every entry multiple built off that period. If you are underwriting a consumer or industrial importer right now, or a healthcare distributor or a med-device name with an unusually good second quarter, the first thing you do is find the tariff footnote and normalize the refund out before you apply a multiple. Getting this wrong means overpaying by the multiple times the refund. That is not a rounding error. That is the deal.

Item one, the Federal Reserve. The Open Market Committee left the target range at three point five zero to three point seven five percent on July twenty-ninth. That is the fifth consecutive hold, and three officials dissented in favor of a twenty-five basis point increase. Not a cut. An increase. June consumer price inflation ran three point five percent year over year, down from four point two in May but still far above the two percent target. July unemployment eased to four point one percent while nonfarm payrolls actually fell by twenty-three thousand. Market-implied odds have flipped from cuts toward hikes ahead of the September fifteenth and sixteenth meeting.

Here is the mechanism, because the dissent looks contradictory at first glance. Dissenting in favor of a hike while payrolls are shrinking only makes sense once you separate demand-driven inflation from supply-driven inflation. If prices are rising because of tariffs and energy costs, that is a supply shock, and raising rates cannot fix the cause. It can only crush demand into an already weakening labor market. The three dissenters are implicitly arguing the opposite case: that three and a half percent inflation this deep into the cycle is no longer a supply story, it is an expectations story, and expectations are the one thing a central bank genuinely controls. That entire debate is compressed into one vote, and it is worth being able to argue both sides.

Now the deep dives.

Deep dive one. The tariff refund, and why the accounting placement is the whole story.

Back on April twentieth, the note in your themes file called the one hundred sixty-six billion dollar refund following the Supreme Court's IEEPA ruling a one-time backdoor corporate stimulus, roughly zero point five five percent of gross domestic product, delivered to importers. Four months later the filings confirm the thesis. The money was real, it went to importers, and it landed fast.

But the April framing missed the part that actually matters to you. It treated this as a macroeconomic event, a stimulus measured against GDP. The August evidence shows it is also a deal-level valuation hazard, and the reason is purely mechanical: refunds flow through cost of goods sold rather than other income. A stimulus that arrived below the operating line would be trivially easy to strip out of a model. A stimulus that arrives inside gross margin is camouflaged.

The historical parallel worth having ready is not Smoot-Hawley, even though tariff conversations reach for it reflexively. The better parallel is the way one-time government transfers during the pandemic distorted small-business earnings for the better part of two years, and the way buyers who did not normalize Paycheck Protection Program forgiveness out of EBITDA systematically overpaid. Same structure: real cash, non-recurring, sitting in a line that models treat as recurring. The firms that caught it early made it a standard diligence question. The firms that did not paid for it at exit.

So the practical version of this insight, the one you can actually use in a conversation: ask whether the quality-of-earnings report normalized the refund. If the seller's advisors left it in, that tells you something about the process. If they took it out, that tells you the seller understands the asset and you should expect the price to reflect it.

Deep dive two. Neoclouds, and the historical rhyme that a sixteen z left out.

The pattern a sixteen z describes is genuinely elegant. Southern Pacific Railroad held rights-of-way far wider than its actual track, so it built a telephone network alongside the rails. Southern Pacific Railroad Internal Networking Telephony. Commercialized in the nineteen seventies. When the long-distance cartel broke and fiber became viable, those corridors were repurposed, and the company became Sprint. In the nineteen eighties, Williams Companies threaded fiber through decommissioned natural gas pipelines to create WilTel, later sold and renamed WorldCom. In the nineteen nineties, one-way cable television coaxial cable was rebuilt into consumer broadband for Comcast and Charter. Today's neoclouds are the same move: crypto-mining operators with power rights, cooling infrastructure, and GPU expertise, repriced for AI compute.

That is a good story. It is also told as three success stories, which is where it becomes misleading.

Sprint was acquired. WorldCom filed the largest bankruptcy in United States history to that point, in two thousand two, and the fiber glut left roughly ninety-five percent of laid capacity dark for years. The infrastructure did survive and it did create enormous value. For the second owner, who bought it at cents on the dollar.

So the actual pattern is not that repurposed infrastructure wins. The pattern is that repurposed infrastructure gets overbuilt on debt, the first equity holder is wiped out, and the asset turns out to be worth a great deal to whoever buys it out of the wreckage. That is a distressed-investing thesis, not a growth-equity one. And it is worth holding against CoreWeave's capital expenditure, its depreciation running above half of revenue, and its rising interest expense.

The framework that organizes this is Three Horizons. Horizon one is the neoclouds' current business: rent GPUs at a spread. Horizon two is managed AI platform services. Horizon three is whatever replaces the GPU rental model entirely. The framework's warning is specific and it applies here exactly: capital-intensive horizon one businesses with heavily depreciating assets rarely generate the free cash flow to fund horizon three, because depreciation and interest consume the cash that reinvention would have required. That is precisely WorldCom's failure mode. And it explains the thing that otherwise looks irrational, which is how a company can grow faster than early AWS and still trade down sixteen percent. The market is not discounting revenue. It is discounting terminal value.

Deep dive three. The agentic AI narrative has crossed from a technical problem into a demand problem, and that is a different investment case.

Track the arc. April fourth: governance is the real bottleneck. April fifth: governance maturity measured at two point three out of five across five hundred organizations. April sixth: DeepMind research showing prompt injection succeeds in up to eighty-six percent of tested scenarios. April seventh: eighty-eight percent of organizations report incidents, but only twenty-two percent treat agents as identities requiring management. April twenty-first: forty-nine percent describe themselves at advanced or expert adoption while ninety-four percent flag agent risk. Today, Schumpeter: buyers are pulling back.

That is seven appearances of the same narrative, and the shape of it has changed. April was a story about missing controls, which is a solvable engineering problem and, if anything, bullish for agent vendors because it implies a roadmap. August is a story about buyers noticing and slowing down. That is demand destruction, and it is a materially worse story for agent capability vendors and a materially better one for governance, identity, audit, and observability vendors.

The structural parallel is Sarbanes-Oxley after Enron. The scandal did not shrink the accounting industry. It created a compliance industry that was larger, stickier, and more recurring than what preceded it. For a healthcare-focused thesis, agent identity and audit tooling is a picks-and-shovels play that does not require you to bet on which model wins, and healthcare is unusually well suited to it because the regulatory surface already exists.

One apparent contradiction worth resolving, because someone will raise it. The Economist says enterprises are pulling back. A sixteen z says the top decile of firms spends fifty times more per employee on AI than the median. Are those in conflict? No. They describe the same bifurcation from opposite sides. Enterprises are retreating from autonomous, high-consequence deployments while increasing spend on supervised, bounded AI. The fifty-times spread is evidence that value is concentrating in the firms that solved the controls problem, not evidence that everyone is spending freely.

Deep dive one. The Dalio stagflation call and what 1979 actually teaches us.

When Dalio says stagflationary period and warns Warsh against cutting, he is not freestyling. He is invoking the most studied case of Fed credibility loss in modern monetary history. In 1979, Paul Volcker replaced G. William Miller after Arthur Burns had spent most of the seventies trying to thread the needle between politically demanded growth and structurally embedded inflation. Burns chose the dovish path. Inflation expectations un-anchored. By the time Volcker took the chair, the only path back to credibility was a recession deep enough to break the wage-price spiral — twenty-percent fed funds rates, two recessions in three years, and a generation of political damage to the Fed. The macro primer I always come back to is this: in a normal regime, cutting rates stimulates demand and you get growth. In a stagflation regime, cutting rates re-anchors inflation expectations higher, the long end of the yield curve resteepens, and you get the worst of both worlds — slower growth and pricier debt. That is why Volcker held. That is why Dalio is now saying do not cut.

Apply McKinsey's framework here. Think of monetary policy in stagflation as a credibility-versus-stimulus tradeoff with a convex payoff. Small cuts have small benefits in growth but disproportionate costs in inflation expectations. The optionality argument — the same one McKinsey teaches in the Strategy Beyond the Hockey Stick canon — says you hold the option when the downside is asymmetric. Warsh's 2010 dissent record says he understands this. The political layer above him may not. That gap is the trade.

For your private equity work, the practical translation is this. Every LBO model written in 2021 or 2022 assumed roughly two-percent inflation and a falling-rate path with a terminal cost of debt around two to three percent. That cohort is the most fragile. The maturity wall hits hardest in 2026 and 2027 for unitranche cov-lite healthcare and consumer LBOs. Add the corporate-practice-of-medicine enforcement layer in California, Oregon, Indiana, and now Vermont, and the lower-middle-market healthcare buyouts compounded by state regulatory risk are the canary. If you are touching a model that still bakes in three cuts in the second half of 2026, the Monday-morning action is the same as it was a month ago — re-run it with one cut. Maybe zero.

Deep dive two. The McKinsey Global Private Markets Report and the Granularity of Growth framework.

Baghai, Coley, and White published The Granularity of Growth in 2007. The thesis is that aggregate growth numbers hide the truth — the only real question is which sub-segments are growing within a decelerating market. Headline minus seventeen percent in PE fundraising sounds like a contraction story. Disaggregate by sub-segment and the picture inverts. North American institutional plus eight. European institutional minus forty-one. APAC minus forty-nine. But U.S. semiliquids plus one-hundred-twenty-two percent from ninety-two billion to two hundred four. Secondaries plus forty-eight. The aggregate is a composite of two completely opposite trends. Capital is not leaving the asset class — it is changing the channel through which it enters. The institutional close-end channel is in structural retreat outside North America. The retail-wealth channel and the secondaries liquidity stack are scaling.

Why does this matter to a career? Because where you sit in that stack matters more than which fund you join. If you are building toward a PE seat, ask yourself whether the platform you are targeting is on the institutional close-end side or on the retail-wealth-plus-secondaries side. That is the second-derivative question that separates a 2010s-style career path from a 2030s-style one.

The partner talking point I want you to internalize is this. The bull case for PE in 2026 is not that fundraising recovers. It is that the liquidity stack matures. Secondaries up forty-eight. Semiliquids doubling. GP-led continuation vehicles becoming routine. These are the plumbing of a thirteen-trillion-dollar asset class learning to behave like a real market. The bear case is that semiliquids are the next mis-sold product to retail. Both can be true. Holding the tension is the partner-level move.

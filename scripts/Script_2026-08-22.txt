---

AVA: Mechanism workshop. Why doesn't a buyback lower long yields?

ANDREW: Start from the obvious intuition, which is wrong.

AVA: The intuition is: the government buys back its own bonds, so there are fewer bonds outstanding, so the price goes up and the yield goes down. Supply and demand. Clean.

ANDREW: And the reason it's wrong?

AVA: Because the Treasury has to pay for the buyback, and it pays for it by issuing more debt. Specifically, more short-dated bills. So the total stock of debt doesn't shrink by a dollar. What changes is the maturity profile — you retire long paper and issue short paper. That's a duration swap, not a supply reduction.

ANDREW: Define duration for anyone who's fuzzy on it.

AVA: Duration is how sensitive a bond's price is to a change in interest rates, and it scales with how far away the cash flows are. A thirty-year bond has a lot of duration. A three-month bill has almost none. When the Treasury swaps long for short, it takes interest-rate risk off the market's books and puts refinancing risk onto its own — those bills come due in months and get rolled at whatever rate prevails then. That's the government shortening the maturity of its liabilities exactly when it needs long-term funding certainty.

ANDREW: Now the term premium, because that's the actual variable here.

AVA: Right. Break a long yield into two pieces. Piece one is the market's average expectation of short rates over the life of the bond — that's the Fed's territory. Piece two is the term premium: the extra compensation investors demand for the risk of holding long paper instead of rolling bills. The term premium is where fiscal credibility lives.

ANDREW: And it's rising.

AVA: It's rising, and a buyback doesn't touch either of its drivers. Driver one is supply — the deficit is stuck near six percent of GDP. Bessent had said it would fall below four. Driver two is uncertainty about the Fed's reaction function under a new chair who hasn't told anyone what it is. A buyback addresses neither. It just moves paper around.

ANDREW: Then why did yields fall at all on Wednesday?

AVA: Short covering. Traders were positioned for higher long yields. The announcement surprised them, they bought to close, yields fell. That's a positioning move, not a repricing. Once the covering was done, nothing had changed and the market went back to where it was.

ANDREW: The quote of the week is on this, and it's not from a policymaker.

AVA: Brent Donnelly at Spectra. He said: "You create the impression of a backstop, but you're not really committed enough." That's the whole problem in nineteen words. The Treasury has now taught the market that it will do something when yields get uncomfortable, without saying at what level or in what size. That's a free option, and markets test free options. They always do.

ANDREW: Is there a bull case?

AVA: There is, and it's Friday. Kevin Warsh speaks at Jackson Hole on August twenty-eighth. JPMorgan's Priya Misra has laid out the bar he has to clear: say concretely that the inflation data doesn't justify a hike yet, but that he wouldn't hesitate if it did. That's a reaction function. If he supplies one, the term premium can compress on its own and the Treasury never needs the buyback. If he says "task force" again, the market runs out of patience.

ANDREW: Which is the thing I want to flag. This is now the highest-information single event of the year for a bond investor, and it is one week away.

ANDREW: Historical parallel, and I want you to be honest about where it breaks.

AVA: The debasement trade turned fifty-five this month. On August fifteenth, nineteen seventy-one, Nixon closed the gold window. A government facing deficits it wouldn't cut and a currency it couldn't defend changed the rules rather than the fiscal position. Gold went from thirty-five dollars an ounce to over eight hundred by January nineteen eighty. The ten-year went from about six percent to fifteen point eight by September nineteen eighty-one. The sequence broke only when a Fed chairman was willing to cause a recession to restore credibility.

ANDREW: Now break it.

AVA: It strains badly on levels. Inflation in seventy-one was accelerating toward double digits and the US was defending a fixed exchange rate it couldn't honor. Today CPI is around three and a half percent and falling, the dollar floats, and a thirty-year at five point three is roughly its nineteen-nineties average. Not a crisis level. Anyone telling you it's nineteen seventy-one is selling gold.

ANDREW: So what survives the comparison?

AVA: The institutional sequence, and only that. A fiscal authority reaches for a technical fix. The market prices the fix as evidence of unwillingness rather than as a solution. And the burden of credibility lands on the central bank, which is the only institution that can actually supply it. TD's Gennadiy Goldberg described this week as "an erosion of confidence in institutions — including the central bank, including Congress, including the Treasury." That is the nineteen seventy-one diagnosis almost verbatim, at one-tenth the magnitude.

ANDREW: And Greg Ip has the quieter version of the same story.

AVA: Two new studies find investors are less willing to accept low yields in exchange for holding Treasurys. That's the convenience yield compressing — the discount the United States has enjoyed purely for issuing the world's reserve asset. If it's shrinking, every risk asset on earth reprices off a permanently higher floor. That's not a week's story. That's a decade's.

ANDREW: Healthcare. Moderna, and the version that ran today is much better than Wednesday's.

AVA: The origin story is the part people will repeat. In twenty thirteen, Moderna had fewer than a hundred employees and had deliberately decided oncology was too hard. Stéphane Bancel told an investor named Patrick Degorce twice that the company couldn't afford it. Degorce, whose wife had died of lung cancer, mailed him a personal check for five hundred thousand dollars. That paid for two cancer scientists.

ANDREW: And thirteen years later.

AVA: Merck put in two hundred million in twenty sixteen. A twenty twenty-two readout showed a forty-four percent reduction in the risk of melanoma recurrence or death versus Keytruda alone. This week's trial hit both endpoints — preventing recurrence and preventing metastasis. The stock nearly tripled. Forty-four billion dollars of market value added in a name that had fallen more than ninety percent from its five-hundred-dollar peak and had twenty percent short interest in June.

ANDREW: This is where you tell me not to enjoy it.

AVA: I'd like you to enjoy it and also count. Three things are unresolved. First: they have not disclosed the effect size or what fraction of patients responded. That comes at a conference later this year. Statistical significance tells you the effect probably isn't zero. It does not tell you whether it's forty-four percent or four. Drew Weissman at Penn — who won the Nobel for the underlying mRNA work, so this is not a skeptic with an axe — said, "We don't know how long it's going to last. The game isn't over yet." Durability is entirely unaddressed.

ANDREW: Second.

AVA: Second, mechanically, part of that move is a squeeze. Twenty percent of the float was short. A binary positive readout in a heavily shorted stock produces forced covering that looks identical, on the day, to fundamental repricing. We will not know the ratio for months.

ANDREW: Third.

AVA: Third, the economics are unpriced and the regulator is hostile. Every dose is bespoke — sequence the patient's tumor, select up to thirty-four targets, synthesize a personalized mRNA strand. That is the most manufacturing-intensive modality anyone has proposed at scale. Bancel has only committed to pricing below CAR-T, and CAR-T runs to five hundred thousand dollars a patient. That's a ceiling, not a price. Meanwhile HHS under Kennedy has cancelled hundreds of millions of dollars of mRNA funding, and this drug needs approval from that same government.

ANDREW: Steel-man the bull side for me, because I don't want this to be a hit piece.

AVA: The honest bull case is that none of that says the science is wrong. It says the distribution of outcomes widened rather than resolved. A stock down ninety percent with a fifth of its float short was priced for the technology to fail outright. It demonstrably has not. Repricing from "fails" to "unknown but plausible" can absolutely be worth forty-four billion dollars.

ANDREW: And the investable question isn't Moderna.

AVA: It isn't. Bancel's own line is the tell: "If it's a five million dollar dose, let's not waste time." If personalized vaccines work, the constraint is sequencing throughput, bespoke manufacturing, and cold chain — the picks-and-shovels tier. Which connects straight to KKR taking Integer Holdings private on August fourteenth at five point seven billion, a fifty-two percent premium. Medtech outsourcing as infrastructure. Sponsors have been underwriting that thesis for a year, and this is the first readout that makes it look early rather than crowded.

ANDREW: AI, and this is the one that might matter most in five years and least this quarter.

AVA: World models. Christopher Mims in the Journal. These are systems trained on videogames and simulations rather than text, built to navigate three-dimensional space and manipulate objects. General Intuition is closing a round at more than six billion dollars — the most valuable lab of its kind. It trains on millions of hours of human gameplay captured by its sister service, Medal dot TV, which links every button press to its consequence.

ANDREW: Action-labelled data.

AVA: Which is the moat, and notice what it isn't. It isn't a data center. Emulate, founded by the ex-DeepMind lead on the Genie world models, is raising over five hundred million. Fei-Fei Li's World Labs bought a robotics firm. Yann LeCun's AMI Labs is exploring architectures outside large language models entirely. And the maturity stamp, from investor Moritz Baier-Lentz: "This is like GPT-two." Which is to say, twenty nineteen. Six billion dollars on a twenty-nineteen-stage technology.

ANDREW: There's a nineteen eighty-eight paper that explains why this is hard, and I think it's the most useful thing on today's list.

AVA: Moravec's paradox. Hans Moravec at Carnegie Mellon observed that high-level reasoning requires very little computation, while low-level sensorimotor skill requires enormous amounts. Chess fell to machines in ninety-seven. Picking up an unfamiliar object on an uneven surface is still hard in twenty twenty-six. His explanation was evolutionary: sensorimotor competence is a billion years of optimization; abstract reasoning is a thin recent veneer.

ANDREW: And the commercial version of the same point.

AVA: George Konidaris at Brown: "The real world is very complex, and has a lot of special cases and hard edges, and you can't approximately miss something." That's the whole thing. Large language models are commercially viable because we tolerate hallucination in prose. Robots have no equivalent tolerance band. A ninety-five percent accurate paragraph is useful. A ninety-five percent accurate forklift is a lawsuit.

ANDREW: What binds first — capital or physics?

AVA: Both, on different clocks. If sensorimotor competence is genuinely a decade problem, a pre-revenue GPT-two-stage architecture is being funded from exactly the capital pool repricing violently in the bond market right now. The tell is who leads the next rounds: strategic robotics buyers survive a funding winter with a product roadmap attached; generalist growth funds mark down and stop.

ANDREW: And one thing not to get wrong.

AVA: These models currently pilot quadrupeds, wheeled vehicles and drones — anything you could control with a gamepad and a video feed. They explicitly exclude most humanoid robots. So the humanoid narrative and the world-model narrative are not the same trade. Conflating them is how people will lose money on this. McKinsey's framing is the useful one: humanoids get the headlines, but the software, data and orchestration layer could be worth three hundred thirty billion by twenty thirty-five. General Intuition is a pure bet on the layer, not the machine.

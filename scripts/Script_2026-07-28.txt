Now the deep dives. Three of them.

Deep dive one: the AI value creation ladder, and why an economic historian from nineteen ninety explains this week better than any analyst note.

Start with the puzzle. Two years of unprecedented AI investment, and the market just erased nearly nine hundred billion dollars of value from the companies spending the most, while McKinsey's data says most corporate AI adoption earns no valuation premium at all. Is AI failing? The historical answer is no — this is the most predictable phase of a general-purpose technology, and it has a name: the productivity paradox. Economic historian Paul David showed in nineteen ninety that American factories needed roughly forty years after electrification began before productivity gains showed up in the data. The reason is that the first generation of factory owners bolted electric motors onto layouts designed for steam — one giant central shaft, machines arranged around it. The gains only arrived when a new generation redesigned the factory around the technology: single-story buildings, machines arranged by workflow, each with its own motor. Adoption is cheap. Reorganization is expensive, slow, and where all the value lives.

Map that onto McKinsey's ladder and the fit is almost exact. Levels one and two — pilots and operating-model enhancement — are electric motors bolted onto the steam layout. That is why the market prices them at zero premium: they are the table stakes everyone can buy. Levels three and four — product transformation and AI business-building — are the redesigned factory, and they command thirty-one times revenue because they are rare, hard, and defensible. McKinsey's gate for level three is specific, and worth memorizing for any diligence conversation: a proprietary data moat, plus an integrated product, data, and engineering operating model. Absent those two prerequisites, pushing a company up the ladder destroys value rather than creating it.

The partner-level talking point, if this comes up at dinner: everyone is arguing about whether AI is overhyped or underhyped, but that is the wrong axis. The right axis is adoption versus reorganization. The eight hundred ninety billion dollar wipeout is the market repricing adopters. The McKinsey premium is the market pre-paying reorganizers. Both can be true at once, and in fact they have to be — that is exactly what eighteen ninety-five looked like.

Deep dive two: the bond market walks into the Fed meeting with un-anchored expectations.

Set the table. The FOMC starts meeting today and announces tomorrow. Coming in, the Fed has held at three point five zero to three point seven five percent for four straight meetings. The ten-year Treasury is pushing its twenty twenty-six high. And The Economist's finding — that investor belief in the two percent target is at a low — is the thread that ties it together.

Here is the mechanism, in three steps, because this is the kind of thing you want to be able to explain fluently. Step one: the Fed controls the overnight rate, but the ten-year yield is set by markets, and it decomposes into expected future short rates plus a term premium — the extra compensation for duration risk, of which inflation uncertainty is the biggest component. Step two: when inflation expectations de-anchor, the term premium rises, so long yields can climb even while the Fed stands still — which is precisely what is happening now. Step three: this puts the Fed in a bind with no clean exit. If it cuts to support growth while expectations are un-anchored, the bond market may read it as tolerance for higher inflation and push long yields up further — meaning a cut could actually tighten financial conditions for anyone borrowing at the long end, which is mortgages, corporates, and leveraged buyouts. If it holds or hikes to defend the target, it risks the growth scare that the AI capex slowdown is already hinting at.

That is why tomorrow is less about the decision — a hold is the overwhelming base case — and more about the language. Friday's episode flagged the specific tell, and it stands: does Powell describe the recent oil move as a supply shock the Fed can look through, or does he voice concern about expectations un-anchoring? The first phrasing calms the long end. The second lights it up. One word choice, measurable in basis points.

For the PE lens: a structurally higher ten-year resets the entire private equity return equation. Entry multiples compress because the discount rate is higher. Leverage costs more, so the debt paydown lever weakens. Exit multiples cannot be assumed to expand. What remains is operational value creation — which loops straight back to deep dive one. In a high-rate world, climbing the AI ladder inside portfolio companies is not a nice-to-have; it is close to the only reliable return driver left. The macro story and the AI story are the same story.

Deep dive three: Blackstone, and what retail redemption from private credit actually signals.

The surface story is strength — profit surging on AI investments, inflows into the PE franchise. The structural story is the retail pullback from private credit, and it deserves the full genealogy, because it is the best live example of how a risk thesis matures. Stage one, March: analysis. The reading flagged that direct lending's software exposure — roughly twenty-six percent of portfolios — was underappreciated, with AI-driven seat compression as the mechanism that could impair it. Stage two, last week: earnings evidence. IBM showed that AI spending can be a revenue headwind for seat-based software, not just a cost line. Stage three, now: fund flows. Retail investors are pulling money from the vehicles most exposed to exactly that lending. Analysis became evidence became flows, in roughly four months.

Why retail flows matter more in private credit than in most asset classes: the semi-liquid vehicles that brought retail into private credit promise periodic redemption from an illiquid book. When redemptions accelerate, managers face gates or forced asset sales — and forced sales in an opaque market are how mark-to-model becomes mark-to-market, suddenly and unpleasantly. This is worth filing as a general pattern: in private markets, the flow data is often the earliest honest signal, because valuations are smoothed but redemption requests are not.

The balancing view, so we are not overclaiming: Blackstone's diversification is doing its job — PE inflows offsetting credit softness — and one quarter of retail pullback is not a run. The signal is directional, not existential. But direction is what you trade on, and the direction says the private credit software question has moved from research desks to client portfolios.

Section two. Deep dives.

The first deep dive — and this is the most important historical anchor I have in front of me today — is the Burns-to-Volcker handoff in nineteen seventy-eight and nineteen seventy-nine, as a parallel for today's data-credibility moment.

Quick history. When Arthur Burns left the Federal Reserve in March nineteen seventy-eight, he left under accusations of having politically accommodated the Nixon administration's preference for easier money. The market — not just the Fed itself — repriced the credibility of United States inflation data in the months that followed. Real yields rose roughly one hundred and fifty basis points in nine months, before Paul Volcker's October nineteen seventy-nine shift to targeting reserves rather than the funds rate. Read that sentence again. The credibility repricing happened in the bond market before the political fight resolved.

Apply that to today. If BLS data integrity is questioned — and the firing of the commissioner makes that question public, regardless of the actual quality of the underlying series — the credibility discount happens in long rates first. The risk is not a single hot CPI print. The risk is a slow erosion of the term premium that nobody on a deal team is currently modeling. Allan Meltzer's history of the Fed, volume two, book two, walks through exactly this dynamic. If you want one piece of bedside reading from today's digest, that is it.

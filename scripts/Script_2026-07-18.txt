Here is the single biggest story, and it is the one everything else bends around. Oil is back as the macro swing factor. On July thirteenth, Brent crude jumped roughly nine and a half percent in a single session to above eighty-three dollars a barrel, its biggest one-day move since May of twenty twenty. The trigger was political, not economic: the United States said it would reimpose a naval blockade on the Strait of Hormuz and charge a twenty percent fee on all cargo transiting the strait, starting July fourteenth, while Iran said it would keep the strait shut. Why does one shipping lane matter so much? Roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes through Hormuz. When you put a toll booth and a blockade on that chokepoint at the same time, you are not raising the price of a commodity, you are raising the price of the entire cost structure of the global economy, because energy sits underneath everything else.

Now let me make sure the mechanism is crisp, because this is exactly the kind of thing that separates a real conversation from a headline recap. This is a supply shock, not a demand shock. Demand inflation is too many dollars chasing too few goods, and a central bank can cool it by raising rates to slow spending. A supply shock is different: raising interest rates cannot create a single additional barrel of oil. So if the Federal Reserve tightens into an oil spike, it slows growth without fixing the thing causing prices to rise. You get weaker growth and higher prices at the same time. That combination has a name, and it is the word every macro conversation this summer keeps circling back to: stagflation. Hold onto that, because it is the lens for the Fed meeting at the end of this month.

That is the sweep. Now three deep dives.

Deep dive one: the oil shock, in historical context. When Hormuz tension flares, the right reflex is to reach for the nineteen seventies. The nineteen seventy-three OPEC embargo removed roughly seven percent of world oil supply and helped trigger the original stagflation. The nineteen seventy-nine shock, after the Iranian revolution, brought Paul Volcker and the most brutal rate-hiking campaign in modern Fed history. But notice what is different this time, because the differences are where the insight lives. First, the United States is now a net energy exporter, so the domestic hit is cushioned in a way it simply was not in nineteen seventy-three. Second, and this is genuinely novel, a twenty percent cargo fee layered on top of a supply cutoff is a hybrid of trade policy and geopolitics. It is a tariff wearing a blockade's clothing. The lesson worth respecting from nineteen seventy-nine is about central-bank psychology: policymakers who misread a supply shock as demand inflation over-tighten and deepen the downturn. Arthur Burns is remembered for being too soft; Volcker is remembered for the recession he caused breaking inflation's back. Today's Fed is trying to thread between those two ghosts. And this weekend's move, Brent around eighty-three, is real but smaller than the roughly one hundred thirty-two dollars you were reading about in the March and April Hormuz scare. So the thread is back, but at lower amplitude. That nuance is the whole game: same story, smaller shock, into a more hawkish Fed.

One more piece of context that makes the oil story sharper. The reason a supply shock is so uncomfortable for markets is that it is bad for both stocks and bonds at the same time, which breaks the usual diversification you rely on. Higher energy costs compress corporate margins, which is bad for equities. And higher inflation pushes the Fed toward tighter policy and higher yields, which is bad for bonds. The classic sixty-forty portfolio, sixty percent stocks and forty percent bonds, is built on the assumption that when one falls the other cushions it. A supply shock is precisely the scenario where both fall together, which is exactly what made twenty twenty-two so painful. So when you hear an oil spike shrugged off as a one-day headline, the real question to ask is whether it forces the Fed's hand, because that is the channel that turns a commodity move into a portfolio problem. For a private-market investor the translation is direct: a higher-for-longer rate path raises your cost of capital and lowers the multiple you can justify paying, at the same moment that energy and supply costs are squeezing the margins of the businesses you own, especially healthcare providers who cannot quickly pass costs through. That is the dual-pressure case, and it is why this is not just a trading story.

Deep dive two: McKinsey's argument on where the AI edge actually comes from. Their July piece, Winning the Race to Rewire, makes a claim worth memorizing because it cuts against the hype. The durable advantage in AI is not the model. It is redesigning how work gets done and how decisions get made, end to end. The framework is called Rewired, built around six capabilities, and the core idea is operating-model redesign: people, process, and decision rights, with the technology as an enabler rather than the headline. Here is the partner-level talking point, and it is a good one to have loaded: everyone benchmarks their AI spend; the winners benchmark their decision latency. The alpha in twenty twenty-six is not a better model, it is who rewires the operating model fastest so the model actually changes what people do. For your world specifically, this is the operating-partner playbook stated plainly. It is the how behind the healthcare-AI return-on-investment numbers McKinsey has published elsewhere: value only shows up when a sponsor rewires the post-close profit-and-loss, not when the company buys one more point solution. And there is a mirror image on the threat side, which brings us to the third dive.

Deep dive three: agentic AI and value migration, seen from two directions at once. Gartner projected that up to two hundred thirty-four billion dollars of enterprise application-software spending is at risk from agentic AI through twenty thirty, what they call agentic arbitrage, where AI agents collapse multi-seat workflows into a single automated flow. The threat there is to the seat-based software-as-a-service business model itself. Now hold that next to McKinsey's rewire argument. They are the same thesis from opposite ends. Gartner measures the value being destroyed; McKinsey describes who captures the value that migrates. The model layer commoditizes. The enablement layer, the operating model that decides how agents are actually deployed, is where the money accrues. If you can hold both of those in one sentence at a dinner, you sound like you understand the structure of the market rather than the ticker of the week. And the same logic shows up in McKinsey's banking work: they estimate that banks that fail to rewire risk roughly one hundred seventy billion dollars, about nine percent of the industry profit pool, over the decade, while the AI pioneers could add up to four percentage points of return on tangible equity. Rewire or lose the profit pool, applied to a second industry.

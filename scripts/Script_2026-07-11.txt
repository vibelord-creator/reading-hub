One mechanism worth banking before we move on, because it's the puzzle sitting underneath today's IPO. How does the biggest listing in US history clear in a world where the Fed might still hike and money costs four percent? The instinct is that high rates should shut the IPO window, and usually they do, so what gives? The answer is that a company's value is the present value of its future cash flows, discounted back at a rate tied to the cost of capital. When rates rise, that discount rate rises, and far-off cash flows get marked down harder, which is why unprofitable, long-duration growth names get hurt most when rates climb. But a genuinely scarce asset with near-term, hard-to-replicate cash flows, HBM that you literally cannot buy from anyone else, has a numerator growing fast enough to swamp the fatter discount rate. So the lesson generalizes cleanly: higher-for-longer doesn't close the exit window for everyone equally. It closes it for the speculative and long-dated, and leaves it wide open for the scarce and cash-generative.

Deep dive one: why the McKinsey healthcare AI fifty percent finding is a real inflection point, not a survey artifact. A year ago, the comparable McKinsey survey put this number at twenty percent — meaning adoption has roughly tripled in twelve months. Sixty-four percent reporting positive ROI is the part that distinguishes this cycle from the dot-com analog people will instinctively reach for. In 1999 to 2001, enterprises spent on internet adoption with little measurable ROI for the first wave; in 2025 to 2026, healthcare AI is producing measurable ROI in revenue cycle, ambient scribing, prior authorization, and utilization management before the architecture has settled. McKinsey's argument — that the next twelve to eighteen months bifurcate point-tool buyers from modular architecture builders — has a precedent: 2010 to 2013 in retail e-commerce, when the firms that bought best-of-breed point solutions for search, recommendations, and checkout fell behind the firms that built composable stacks. Walmart Labs versus retailers buying Endeca and ATG was the visible split. Apply that to healthcare. The provider that buys an ambient scribe from one vendor, a prior-authorization assistant from another, and a CDI tool from a third is the equivalent of the retailer that bought Endeca in 2011 — they will spend two more cycles on integration debt before they can compound. The provider that builds a modular agent platform — even using third-party agents — pulls ahead because the marginal cost of adding a new agent collapses. For PE, the implication is that the targets to underwrite are platform companies that abstract orchestration, not vertical AI tools that solve one workflow. That is a different shape of investment thesis than most healthcare AI funds are running today.

Deep dive two: the Foundayo launch and what it actually says about the GLP-1 reset. The first-week prescription gap is the wrong number to fixate on. Three structural facts matter more. One, the price points. Twenty-five dollars per month with commercial coverage, one hundred forty-nine dollars self-pay, two hundred forty-five dollars Medicare — the entire range now sits below three hundred for the first time. Two, the channel. Amazon's parallel push to three thousand cities by mid-year, four thousand five hundred by year-end, introduces a third channel that did not exist for injectable launches. Three, the molecule. Foundayo is a small molecule, which means it is orally stable, dramatically cheaper to manufacture, and does not need cold-chain logistics. That changes the global TAM more than the US headline numbers suggest — emerging markets where injectable cold chain was never viable now become addressable. The McKinsey framework to apply here is the Value Creation Framework, specifically the question of which players have pricing power to keep volume benefit despite ASP compression versus which give the margin back. Lilly has volume leverage — Foundayo, Mounjaro, Zepbound, and the oral pipeline mean total units more than offset ASP decline. Novo's 2026 outlook already guides for sales decline; reset economics accelerate that. For mid-tier oral GLP-1 targets — Viking, Structure — the demand-side gets better, the pricing-side gets worse, and the M&A premium for differentiated mechanisms rises. The compounded-semaglutide and specialty-pharmacy segment is going to compress violently inside eighteen months. That is the cleanest short-thesis setup in healthcare PE for 2026.

Deep dive three: the historical parallel for today's stagflation debate. Brent at one hundred six, CPI at three point three, Fed cuts pushed to late 2026 — this is the structural shape of the 1979 oil shock. The textbook case is Volcker taking the Fed funds rate to twenty percent in 1981 and triggering the deepest recession since the Depression to break inflation expectations. The lesson the 2022 to 2026 generation of central bankers internalized is that supply-driven inflation behaves differently from demand-driven inflation, and tightening into a supply shock can compound the damage. McKinsey's Geopolitical Value at Stake framework — revenue times geopolitical distance times severity — is the modern actionable answer. The way you use it is to pick a target portfolio company, score its revenue by geography on UN voting alignment distance from the home jurisdiction, then layer the severity score for current trade tensions. Anything where the resulting GVaS number exceeds fifteen percent of revenue is the conversation you take to the operating partner before month-end. The intellectual lineage is Pierre Wack's scenario planning at Shell in the 1970s, formalized in his 1985 Harvard Business Review piece, which is the direct ancestor of McKinsey's GVaS today. The historical lesson: firms that built scenario planning capability during the 1973 to 1976 window compounded competitive advantage for two decades. The window for building GVaS capability is measured in the same months the 1970s firms had to build scenario planning. First-movers in 1974 ran exposure screens their competitors were not running until 1985. That is the pattern that repeats.

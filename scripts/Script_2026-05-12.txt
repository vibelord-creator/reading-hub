Deep dive one. The CPI print today triggered something the market has not really had to deal with since the early 1980s: a moment where the Fed's credibility itself is the binding constraint on policy. The historical parallel is the Volcker Shock, from 1979 to 1982. When Paul Volcker took the Fed chair in August 1979, inflation was at seven point seven percent and still rising. Volcker hiked the fed funds rate from an average of eleven point two percent in 1979 to twenty percent by June 1981. The prime rate hit twenty-one and a half percent. The United States suffered two recessions before inflation actually broke, from fourteen point eight percent in March 1980 to under three percent by 1983.

But here is Volcker's central insight, as the St. Louis Fed documented in a retrospective: markets had observed the Fed's lack of fortitude in the previous decade. They had no expectations that the Fed would actually conquer inflation. Volcker had to prove that the Fed would take the heat, two recessions and twenty-one percent prime rates, before inflation expectations re-anchored. Credibility was the constraint, not the policy mechanics. The lesson, in Volcker's framing, was that tough now is cheaper than tougher later.

Now translate to today. CPI at three point eight, the market suddenly pricing a possible hike, Ray Dalio calling stagflation, Bank of America moving the first cut to July 2027. The 2026 Fed is facing a Volcker-style credibility test in miniature. Powell, or whoever succeeds him, cannot be seen as cutting into hot inflation without permanently damaging the institution's anti-inflation reputation. The market knows this. That is why a single CPI print can move the probability of a hike by such a large amount. Investors are not pricing the data. They are pricing the credibility-preservation incentive of the central bank.

For your investment thinking, the practical implication is that LBO discount-rate distributions just got fatter on both tails. A sponsor modeling a base case that glides to three percent now has to weight a regime where the Fed actually goes the other direction. That changes capital structure choices, hurdle rates, and how you stress test a five-year hold.

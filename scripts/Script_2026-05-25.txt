Deep dive one. Nineteen seventy-nine. The Volcker pivot, forty-seven years later. When Paul Volcker took over from G. William Miller in August nineteen seventy-nine, fed funds was eleven percent and inflation was eleven point eight percent. Miller had cut into an oil shock and lost credibility. Volcker hiked to twenty percent by March nineteen eighty, triggered back-to-back recessions, cost four million American jobs, and broke inflation expectations. Unemployment peaked at ten point eight percent in late nineteen eighty-two. CPI collapsed from a peak of fourteen and a half percent in early nineteen eighty to below three percent by nineteen eighty-three. Volcker bought the Fed forty years of credibility — credibility Powell got to spend down across twenty twenty-one through twenty twenty-five. Warsh's first ninety days define whether twenty twenty-six through twenty twenty-eight is another credibility win or another Miller episode. The lesson — under stagflation, inflation expectations depend on whether markets believe the central bank will accept pain. Pre-Volcker, the Fed had threatened tightening multiple times under Burns and Miller and backed off every time. Markets stopped listening. By the time Volcker arrived, the only path back to credibility was demonstrating willingness to accept the four million job losses. Today's bond market move is the modern version of the same conversation. Bond traders are saying — we have heard the easing bias for a year, we do not believe you. Deep dive two. McKinsey's Three Horizons framework, applied to provider-led health plans. Three Horizons is a classic McKinsey framework — Horizon One is the existing core business, Horizon Two is the emerging business that builds on core capabilities, and Horizon Three is the genuinely new business that creates optionality. Apply it to the PLHP world. Horizon One — the existing Medicare Advantage book, where most provider plans were built and where they have scale. Horizon Two — commercial group extension, the twenty-billion-dollar pool McKinsey just quantified. Horizon Three — direct-to-employer contracting platforms, where the system bypasses the broker and the carrier and sells directly to a self-insured employer. Most provider-led plans are stuck in Horizon One because the operating muscle for Horizon Two — broker relationships, employer-facing P-and-L, network design for commercial group — is genuinely different from MA. The PE play is to underwrite the H1-to-H2 transition explicitly, with operating expertise embedded at the platform level. The IRR uplift comes from the operating sweat, not the multiple. Combine this with the integration-taxonomy article — the framework is — Horizon Two is where the integration playbook lives. The roll-ups that work in twenty twenty-six are PLHP platforms acquiring commercial-broker relationships and TPA capabilities. The combinations that work are MA plans pairing with regional commercial carriers. The capabilities deals are MSO-style shared-services builds. McKinsey gave you both the targeting framework and the playbook in the same week.

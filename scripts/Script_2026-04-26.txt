Horizon one is the mature buyout core — large-cap and mega buyout, traditional LBOs, the long-running franchise. Horizon two is the growth engine — private credit, GP-led secondaries, infrastructure. GP-led secondaries went from twenty-five billion in 2018 to roughly one hundred fifty billion in 2025 — a six-times growth in seven years. The pattern that repeats is that whoever maps capital flows to McKinsey's emerging vocabulary first, captures category leadership before the consensus catches up. For the PE associate, the punchline is that careers in the 2026 to 2030 vintage will be made in Horizon two — private credit specifically, GP-led secondaries broadly, and the retail wealth channel for the patient few. Deep dive three: the 1973 to 1974 historical parallel for today's stagflation debate. In 1973 the OPEC embargo removed roughly four point five million barrels per day from the global market, triggering a four-times oil price spike, double-digit US inflation, and the brutal 1973 to 1975 recession. The Hormuz scenario flagged earlier this month — twenty million barrels per day at risk — would dwarf 1973 by four times in volume if it materialized in full. Brent at one hundred six versus 1973's spike from three to twelve dollars looks small in absolute terms, but as a percentage of global GDP the impact is comparable because oil intensity per dollar of GDP has dropped. McKinsey's 1976 retrospective concluded the corporate winners of the 1970s were companies that did three things early. One — they locked in long-term supplier contracts before competitors did. Two — they accelerated automation and labor productivity to defend margin. Three — counterintuitively, they raised prices to defend margin, even in a recession, because that was the only way to fund reinvestment. The PE corollary today is that operating partners should be running the same three plays inside portfolio companies right now. Lock in supplier contracts where leverage exists. Push automation deployment forward by six to twelve months versus plan. And take pricing where the market will bear it, even in soft consumer segments — because price restraint compounds into margin destruction faster than volume gain at flat price compounds into earnings. The McKinsey 1970s playbook is reportedly being dusted off inside several PE operating teams right now. The intellectual lineage is direct.

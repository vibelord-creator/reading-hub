Story one, and our Must Read, is from Telis Demos at the Wall Street Journal: how a tight-lipped Fed could lead to higher mortgage rates. The argument is elegant. Warsh has deliberately drained forward guidance out of FOMC communication. The statement is shorter, the signals are fewer. But markets used that guidance to price duration, to figure out where rates were headed over the life of a long-dated bond. Take the signal away, and lenders don't just shrug. They demand compensation for the uncertainty. That compensation is the term premium, the extra yield investors require to hold longer-maturity debt rather than rolling short-term paper. Think of it this way: if you lend money for thirty years instead of overnight, you want to be paid for everything that could go wrong over those thirty years, and the less the Fed tells you about its intentions, the bigger that unknown becomes. A fatter term premium pushes 30-year mortgage rates up even though the policy rate itself hasn't moved, because mortgage rates are priced off long-dated Treasury yields, and those yields carry the term premium directly. The irony Demos lands on is sharp: a Fed trying to project discipline can tighten financial conditions through sheer silence. So what for you? The risk-free rate plus the term premium is the denominator in every leveraged buyout model and every real estate model you will ever touch. If guidance-light policy fattens that term premium, financing costs rise for healthcare private equity deals regardless of where the funds rate actually sits. This is FNCE 6110 material made live: cost of capital and the discount rate, set not by the headline rate but by the premium markets attach to uncertainty. If you want a line for a networking conversation, try this: Warsh's communication style is itself a monetary tool. Less forward guidance means a wider term premium, so he can tighten mortgages without ever touching the funds rate.

Let me give you the strategic context, because today has two genuinely good historical parallels.

The first is about leverage-fueled rallies, and the years to hold in your head are 1929, 1999, and 2026. US margin debt just hit a record of roughly 1.42 trillion dollars. That's up eight and a half percent in a single month, and up 53 percent year over year. Margin debt is simply money investors borrow against their portfolios to buy more stock. It echoes the margin-loan booms that preceded both the 1929 crash and the 1999-to-2000 dot-com peak. The mechanism is identical across all three: leverage amplifies your gains on the way up, and then forces synchronized, simultaneous liquidations on the way down, because when prices fall, everyone gets a margin call at once and is forced to sell into the same falling market. Here's the nuance, and it's important: the lesson is not that a crash is imminent. Record leverage is a terrible market-timing tool. What it actually does is raise the convexity of any correction, meaning it makes whatever drawdown does come sharper and more violent, because the selling feeds on itself. This is risk management, not a prediction.

The second parallel is a McKinsey classic applied to AI: the Three Horizons framework, from The Alchemy of Growth by Baghai, Coley, and White. The seven-operating-truths gap maps cleanly onto it. Horizon one is today's AI tooling, the 88 percent with a chatbot. Horizon two is rewiring an individual function's operating model. Horizon three is becoming genuinely AI-native across the whole enterprise. The roughly 1 percent of mature firms are funding horizons two and three, while everyone else is stuck optimizing horizon one. If you want to sound like you've thought about this for a while, that's the structure: most firms are trapped in H1, and the value is in who's willing to fund H2 and H3.

Let me sit with that Economist comparison for a moment, because the question of whether 2026 could rhyme with 2008 is worth understanding mechanically, not just as a scary headline. In 2008, the fragility lived inside the regulated banking system: banks held subprime mortgage exposure, often off-balance-sheet, and when it soured, the contagion ran through institutions that were supposed to be the safe core of the system. The reason today is different in shape is that a lot of the risk has migrated out of banks and into private credit, the world of direct lending funds, business-development companies, and non-bank lenders that has ballooned over the past decade. That migration was partly by design, because post-crisis regulation pushed risky lending off bank balance sheets, and partly the hunt for yield in a long low-rate era. The optimistic read is that private credit is funded by long-dated, locked-up capital, so it can't suffer a classic bank run where depositors flee overnight. The pessimistic read, and the one the Economist is gesturing at, is that nobody has actually seen how this system behaves in a deep default cycle, because it grew up entirely during a benign one. The honest answer is we don't know, and that uncertainty is itself the point. When you pair stretched valuations, record margin debt, and an untested private-credit layer, you don't get a prediction of a crash, you get a system with more hidden interconnections and less visibility than the one that broke in 2008. For someone underwriting healthcare deals increasingly financed by exactly this private-credit machine, that's not abstract. It's the plumbing your future deals will run through.

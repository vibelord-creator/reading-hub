Second: Kevin Warsh made his public case for Fed rate cuts over the weekend, and the market noticed. Warsh — widely viewed as the Trump administration's leading candidate for Fed chair — argued that AI-driven productivity has structurally raised potential GDP and the neutral real rate, which means current policy is more restrictive than the headline funds rate suggests. Sitting FOMC members are skeptical. Stocks sit at record highs. Bond investors, notably, have not joined the party — ten-year Treasury yields remain elevated. The so-what: the AI-productivity-offset argument is becoming the intellectual cover for pre-cutting rates in a period where the inflation data may not actually cooperate. This matters more than it sounds — it matters for every LBO on your desk. A fifty to seventy-five basis point move in terminal rate expectations changes cap rates, debt service coverage, and exit multiples. If you touched a model that assumed a 2026 cutting cycle of two or three cuts, you probably need to run the Warsh scenario — which is three to four cuts — and the Powell-successor-holds-the-line scenario — which is zero or one — as explicit branches, because those two outcomes produce very different IRRs.

A quick primer inline, since this comes up often. The Fed's neutral rate — what economists call r-star — is the short rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. If AI has genuinely lifted productivity growth by half a percentage point, r-star moves up by roughly the same amount, which means a four-and-a-quarter percent funds rate is actually less restrictive than the staff models assume. That is the entire Warsh thesis in one sentence. If it is right, you can cut without re-igniting inflation. If it is wrong — and the productivity shows up in the narrative before it shows up in total factor productivity data — you are cutting into a Hormuz oil shock, and that is the 1970s Burns-era playbook nobody wants to repeat.

Third: the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA-based tariff regime, and one hundred sixty-six billion dollars in refunds begins processing Monday morning. That is roughly zero-point-five-five percent of US GDP landing as a one-time corporate windfall over the next several quarters. Legally, the refund goes to the importers of record who paid the duty. Economically, the burden was passed through to end consumers as higher prices — which means customers effectively lose twice. Once through tariff pass-through on the way up, and once again by not receiving a refund. The so-what: this is the largest backdoor corporate stimulus of the decade, and the political fallout has not yet started. Watch retail, consumer durables, and industrial importers for unexpected Q2 margin beats. On the political side, expect a legislative effort by fall to claw a portion back or redirect it, which is a headline risk for any 2026 IPO or secondary with tariff-sensitive revenue.

GVAS — pronounced like the word — extends the classic Value at Risk framework into trade, tariff, sanctions, and security exposure. The argument is that executives must quantify the share of enterprise value tied to geopolitical shifts at the business-unit level and then build the organizational agility to reallocate capital in weeks, not quarters. The so-what: this is the framework that PE deal teams are going to adopt for 2026 diligence in a post-tariff-refund, Hormuz-risk world. Expect GVAS language to appear in IC memos by Q3. Partner talking point, if you are at a networking event this week: the firms that win the next cycle will not be the ones with the best macro forecasts, but the ones that quantified geopolitical exposure at the sub-segment level and built the organizational muscle to move capital quickly. That is the news sweep. Let me now go deeper on the three stories that most shape this week's decisions.

Deep dive one: the Hormuz risk premium and why it is different this time. The 1973 OPEC embargo removed roughly four-point-five million barrels per day from global supply — about seven percent of the market at the time. It triggered a decade of stagflation, wage-price spirals, and ultimately the Volcker rate regime that broke the back of inflation expectations. A full closure of the Strait of Hormuz today would remove approximately twenty million barrels per day — more than four times the 1973 shock — and it would arrive into an economy already carrying elevated debt-to-GDP, a fiscal deficit that cannot absorb another demand-side stimulus, and an AI productivity narrative that has not yet shown up in measured total factor productivity data. The 1973 playbook — supply rationing, forced efficiency, eventual monetary tightening — is the template to stress-test against. McKinsey's Granularity of Growth framework, from the 2008 Baghai, Smit, and Viguerie book, is the right tool here: disaggregate the exposure sub-segment by sub-segment before allocating capital. Which of your portfolio companies have diesel pass-through clauses? Which have hedged one or two quarters out, not four? Which rely on emerging-market end customers whose currency will move against them in a dollar-strength scenario? Those questions, asked at the business-unit level, are exactly what GVAS formalizes.

Deep dive two: the Warsh Fed rate-cut case and the bond market's quiet objection. The cleanest way to hold both ideas in your head is to notice that the equity market and the bond market are pricing two incompatible worlds. Equities are pricing an AI-productivity regime where growth is higher, inflation is contained, and the Fed can cut. Bonds are pricing sticky inflation and a higher-for-longer curve. One of them is wrong. The institutional memory in the Fed — the ghost of Arthur Burns, the 1970s stop-and-go rate cycle that produced the worst inflation of the postwar era — pushes the sitting FOMC toward holding the line. The political memory in the White House — the desire for a dovish chair and accommodative policy into the 2028 cycle — pushes toward Warsh. The frame that connects this to our portfolio: if you are underwriting a 2026 LBO, you need two base cases now, not one. A Powell-holds case with one cut and a Warsh case with three or four. Model both and show the IRR delta to the IC. That is the Monday-morning action item.

Deep dive three: the one hundred sixty-six billion dollar tariff refund as a corporate margin event. The political framing of this is messy because the refund is legally clean — importers paid the duty, they get the refund — but the economic incidence fell on consumers through pass-through pricing. For PE, the useful way to think about it is as a one-time margin tailwind concentrated in a specific set of sectors: consumer discretionary, home improvement, specialty retail, industrials, and select pharma inputs. Some importers will bank the refund. Others will use it to fund price competition, which shows up as multi-quarter margin compression for their smaller competitors. The framework to apply is McKinsey's Value Creation Framework — specifically, the question of which companies have pricing power to keep the windfall versus which will give it back. That is the difference between a one-time EBITDA uplift and a repeatable margin reset. For names with genuine pricing power — category leaders, differentiated brands, duopoly structures — the refund is real equity value. For everyone else, it is a headfake.

That is the sweep. Now two deep dives that you should be able to deliver at dinner tonight with historical context.

Deep dive one. The Volcker shock — what nineteen seventy-nine through nineteen eighty-three actually cost, and why it matters for Warsh today. In October nineteen seventy-nine, Paul Volcker — newly installed as Fed Chair — raised the funds rate to twenty percent. The back-to-back recessions of nineteen eighty and eighty-one to eighty-two cost roughly four million American jobs. The unemployment rate hit ten point eight percent in late nineteen eighty-two — the highest postwar reading until the global financial crisis. But by nineteen eighty-three, CPI inflation had collapsed from a peak of fourteen and a half percent to below three percent. Volcker had reset inflation expectations and earned the Federal Reserve forty years of credibility — the credibility that, by the way, Powell got to spend down across twenty twenty-one through twenty twenty-five. The lesson for Warsh: under stagflation, inflation expectations depend on whether markets believe the central bank will accept pain. Pre-Volcker, the Fed had threatened tightening multiple times under Arthur Burns and G. William Miller — and backed off every time. Markets stopped listening. By the time Volcker arrived, the only way back to credibility was to demonstrate the willingness to accept four million jobs lost. If Warsh signals hikes and follows through, expectations reset cheaply. If he flinches, the path that breaks them later gets steeper. That is the framework. Warsh's first follow-through, or first flinch, will be priced into long rates within hours of the meeting that delivers it.

Deep dive two. McKinsey's Granularity of Growth thesis, applied to healthcare today. The Granularity of Growth was a two thousand seven book by Baghai, Coley, and White — three McKinsey partners — that became the canonical argument for why aggregate sector-level growth numbers are misleading. The core finding: more than sixty percent of value creation at the firm level comes from picking the right sub-segment, not from the macro tailwind in your industry. Apply that lens to healthcare in twenty twenty-six. The aggregate picture is bleak — margins compressing across hospitals, GLP-1 pricing pressure on pharma, payer-mix headwinds. But underneath the aggregate, sub-segments are growing fast. Health-services and technology — HST — is growing at an eight percent revenue CAGR per McKinsey's twenty twenty-six outlook. Provider-led health plans have a twenty-billion-dollar addressable commercial-insurance gap. Primary care has a forty-five-million-American unmet-need backlog. Each of these is a granularity-of-growth argument in twenty twenty-six clothes. The PE playbook: do not bet on sector growth, bet on sub-segment selection. The McKinsey article being published this week is essentially a fresh edition of an eighteen-year-old argument, dressed in healthcare.

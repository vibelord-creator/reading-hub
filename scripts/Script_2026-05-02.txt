A quick definition before we move on, because this term comes up four times this week. Stagflation means simultaneously high inflation and weak growth. It is a Fed nightmare because the standard fix for inflation, which is raising rates, makes growth worse, and the standard fix for weak growth, which is cutting rates, makes inflation worse. The current setup is being called stagflation-lite because growth is slow but not yet negative, and inflation is sticky but not runaway. The Fed can stay on hold rather than acting in either direction, which is exactly what the futures market is now pricing.

A short macro and finance basics aside before deep dive three, because two terms keep coming up. DPI stands for distributions to paid-in capital. It measures how much actual cash an LP has gotten back relative to the cash committed. TVPI stands for total value to paid-in capital, which is DPI plus the unrealized value still sitting in the portfolio. The shift from TVPI to DPI as the headline LP metric is structural. After the 2021 vintage write-ups proved difficult to actually realize, LPs stopped trusting paper marks and started demanding cash. Every fundraising deck right now leads with DPI, not TVPI, and that single change is reshaping how GPs design exits. The second term to know is GP-led secondary. That is a transaction where the existing GP moves an asset out of an aging fund into a new continuation vehicle, with new LPs coming in to provide liquidity to the old LPs. It used to be a workout tool for stuck assets. It is now a strategy.

Deep dive three. The McKinsey Global Private Markets Report 2026, which you read last weekend, is becoming the single best piece of vocabulary for the asset class right now. Three things to memorize. First, dry powder is receding from the 1.3 trillion peak to around 880 billion in US PE alone. Second, fundraising is bifurcating sharply between megafunds and the middle market. The frame is McKinsey's three horizons of private capital. Horizon one is mature buyout, the core. Horizon two is private credit and GP-led secondaries, the growth engine. Horizon three is retail and wealth channels and infrastructure-as-a-service, the experimental bet. Most new LP capital in 2026 is flowing to Horizon two, especially private credit. Horizon three is growing fastest off a small base. That is the geometry of the industry to know cold. Every LP conversation in 2026 starts with show me cash back rather than show me TVPI. The funds that figure out the GP-led secondary playbook and open a credible retail channel will end up with twice the AUM five years from now. Everyone else is going to feel the squeeze.

One more strategic context note before we move to the threads. The historical parallel that should be sitting on your desk this weekend is 1973 to 1974, not 1979. The 1973 OPEC embargo removed roughly four and a half million barrels a day from the global market. It triggered a four-times oil-price spike, double-digit US inflation, and the brutal 1973 to 1975 recession. The Hormuz scenario flagged in your reading puts twenty million barrels a day at risk, which would dwarf 1973 by a factor of four in volume terms. McKinsey's 1976 retrospective concluded the corporate winners of the 1970s were the companies that did three things early. They locked in long-term supplier contracts. They accelerated automation and labor productivity. And, counterintuitively in a recession, they raised prices to defend margin and fund reinvestment. That third move is the partner-level insight. Cutting prices into a supply shock looks defensive but actually erodes the cash needed to retool. Several PE operating teams are reportedly dusting off this exact 1970s playbook right now.

One short add on the McKinsey PE CEOs research from last weekend. Three patterns separated the top performers in their study of one hundred-plus portfolio company CEOs. They installed a one-hundred-day operating cadence with weekly value-creation reviews. They over-invested in finance and human-resources upgrades in the first six months, against the instinct to defer those spends to year two. And they made the toughest organizational calls in the first ninety days, before cultural memory of the prior owner set the tone. The shockingly consistent finding was that top-quartile portfolio companies replaced the chief financial officer in the first ninety days. Bottom-quartile portcos waited for a quarterly miss to force the move. If you walk into a healthcare PE conversation, that is the data point to lead with on operating-partner discipline.

The way to use that vocabulary in a partner conversation is to frame any AI investment thesis as a stack-layer optionality question. Chips, infrastructure, models, data, and applications. Second, on the operational PE versus financial PE distinction that runs through every McKinsey piece this month. If you are positioning for a healthcare PE seat post-EMBA, the recruiting conversation breaks into two camps. One camp is staffed mostly by ex-investment bankers and runs on financial structuring discipline. The other camp is staffed mostly by ex-consultants and operating partners and runs on portfolio company value creation. The Wharton EMBA pipeline historically tilts toward the financial camp, but the data in the McKinsey PE CEOs research and the Beating the Odds exit-readiness study both point in the operating direction. The question to ask in any recruiting conversation is, what does your firm's first hundred days look like inside a portfolio company. If the answer is dominated by financial reporting cadence, you are talking to a financial firm. If the answer is dominated by org calls, finance and human-resources upgrades, and a written value-creation plan, you are talking to an operating firm. Both are real careers. The skill stack is different, and the EMBA is a better preparation for the operating side than for the financial side.

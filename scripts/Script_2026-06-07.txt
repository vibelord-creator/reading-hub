Now the deep dives, where the frameworks live.

First: McKinsey's argument that your budget is killing your strategy. The piece calls traditional annual budgeting "strategically corrosive," and lays out four fixes. One, start from strategy and value-creation potential, not last year's line items. Two, shift 10 to 20 percent of capital year over year toward higher-return opportunities. Three, replace single-scenario forecasts with rolling scenario reviews and explicit contingency governance. Four, use AI and machine learning to drive forward-looking, driver-based planning around just 8 to 10 core variables. A small group of companies has eliminated traditional budgets entirely, replacing them with targets plus automated forecasting. The framework underneath is what McKinsey calls driver-based planning with rolling scenarios, and it sits in the same toolkit as Three Horizons and Granularity of Growth. And here's the older idea it operationalizes: the 2008 McKinsey classic "Granularity of Growth" argued that 80 percent of growth variance comes from segment and sub-segment choices, not corporate-level strategy. The new budgeting piece is the operating version of that — stop funding last year's segments, reallocate to the 10-to-20 percent of cells with disproportionate return. Why does this matter for private equity and healthcare? Because portfolio companies still running annual budget cycles cannot allocate against opportunities that move in agentic-AI quarters. The partner-level point: the most under-priced value-creation lever in PE right now isn't pricing or sourcing — it's killing the annual budget. In a high-rate world, driver-based planning with in-year reallocation is the only way an operating partner actually compounds against the cost of capital.

Second deep dive: the historical parallel the Fed is living inside. The current setup — rates at 3.50 to 3.75, an energy shock, a credibility scare after the BLS commissioner was fired, and an incoming Fed chair in the Powell-to-Warsh transition — maps almost perfectly onto two very different precedents. In 1995, Greenspan held rates briefly, the commodity scare faded, and the Fed cut into what became a five-year expansion. In 1973, Arthur Burns kept policy loose under political pressure as the Yom Kippur war drove oil up fourfold — and the Fed's credibility didn't recover for a decade. Those are the two templates. The June dot plot is the cleanest signal we'll get on which one the committee is running. And the wrinkle that the bond market hasn't fully priced: this is the first meeting since the BLS shakeup, so listen to how Powell talks about data quality in the press conference. That's the Burns-era credibility premium hiding in plain sight.

Fourth, and this is the framework I'd most want you to carry: McKinsey's "Multispeed Banking." In a podcast this week, the firm argued that AI isn't just cutting bank costs — it's redistributing profit pools, redefining customer relationships, and compressing the competitive cycle. The framework names three innovation cadences that have to run in parallel — call them Fast, Faster, and Fastest — because gen AI's roughly two-year adoption curve collapsed the "smart followership" grace period that incumbents relied on through the entire digital era. In plain terms: it used to be safe to be a fast follower, to let someone else take the first arrow and then copy the winning move. AI killed that luxury, because the adoption curve is now too short to catch up from behind. The reason this travels far beyond banking is that the healthcare-payer analogue is exact. The "payvider" model — payers and providers converging — is the same three-speed problem: you have to run your legacy claims business, your digital transformation, and your agentic-AI bets simultaneously, with governance that can handle all three at once. The partner-level translation: the firms that win in 2026, whether banks or payers or providers, aren't the ones with the best AI strategy on paper — they're the ones who built the operating governance to run three innovation cadences at the same time without one starving the others.

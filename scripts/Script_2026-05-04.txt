Here is the single biggest story to anchor on this morning. Jerome Powell is leaving the Federal Reserve board entirely after the April twenty-ninth meeting. Not just stepping down as chair. He is exiting the board itself. Quick context. The Federal Reserve has a seven-member Board of Governors, all appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate to fourteen-year terms. The Chair is one of those governors but holds a separate four-year term as chair. Stepping down as chair is normal at the end of a term. Leaving the board entirely is rare and means the seat opens up for a new appointment, which means the President gets to fill it. The Wall Street Journal frames this exit as a forced choice rather than a personal preference, driven by the legal pressure campaign from the Trump administration. Why this matters at a regime level is that Powell has been the institutional anchor on the Federal Open Market Committee, the FOMC, for eight years. The FOMC is the body that actually sets interest rates. It has twelve voting seats. Seven are board governors, one is the New York Fed President permanently, and four rotate among the other regional Fed presidents. The April twenty-ninth meeting was already a four-way split on the policy decision. One dissent for an actual rate cut, three additional dissents on the easing-bias language. With Powell gone, the next meeting is the first one in the post-anchor era. The dovish path that you have been tracking through the Kevin Warsh productivity argument now lands in a board with much less friction against it.

A quick definition before we move on, because these terms are going to come up four times this week. A Fed four-way split means voting members disagreed on both the rate level and the forward-guidance language at the same meeting. Forward guidance is the paragraph at the end of the policy statement that signals what the committee is likely to do next. Easing-bias language specifically means wording that points toward future rate cuts. So three governors wanted the statement to lean more dovishly than what was published. That is structurally more dovish than a clean hold because the only thing keeping the cut from happening was the chair's institutional weight on the language vote, not a unified hawkish view. Hawkish here means prioritizing fighting inflation. Dovish means prioritizing growth and employment. A regime change in monetary policy means the decision-making setup itself has shifted, not just the votes.

Item two. Total US prescription drug spend will cross one trillion dollars in 2026. The Detroit News and USA Today are reporting that 2025 spend rose roughly thirteen percent to nine hundred and fifteen billion. The number that matters is the composition. Tirzepatide, which is Lilly's Zepbound and Mounjaro, is now the single largest-selling drug in America at roughly sixty-three billion dollars wholesale. Semaglutide, which is Novo's Ozempic and Wegovy, is number two at fifty-nine billion. Both are GLP-1s, a class of drug originally developed for type two diabetes that turned out to drive significant weight loss as a side effect. A consumer-facing weight drug is now the top of the formulary in the United States. The formulary is the list of drugs a payer will reimburse and at what tier. That has never been true before. For healthcare private equity this is the strongest possible signal that GLP-1 adjacencies, including compounding pharmacy, MedSpa, weight management, and cardio-renal services, will see entry-multiple inflation through year-end.

A short pharma basics aside before item three. Wholesale revenue is not net revenue. Drug companies negotiate rebates with payers, and those rebates can be huge. Gross-to-net rebates on GLP-1s run forty to sixty percent. So sixty-three billion wholesale tirzepatide translates to roughly twenty-five to thirty-eight billion net. ASP is average selling price, the volume-weighted price after rebates. That is the number that actually flows to a drug maker's profit line and to investment-committee multiples. The right way to talk about this in an investment committee is in net terms, because that is what flows to multiple.

Item three. McKinsey published its 2025 healthcare revenue cycle management survey. Quick definition. Revenue cycle management, or RCM, is the back-office function inside a hospital or physician practice that handles the entire money-collection process. Patient registration and eligibility checks. Coding the visit into billing codes. Submitting the claim to the insurer. Following up on denials. Posting the cash when it arrives. RCM employs a huge number of people in the US healthcare system and is the single largest controllable cost line for most providers. The headline of the McKinsey survey is that providers are now pairing artificial intelligence and automation deployments with operating model redesign, not running them as parallel programs. McKinsey is naming this the Touchless Revenue Cycle, where claim intake, adjudication, and cash posting are fully automated and the human work moves to exceptions only. The cost-to-collect compression band is thirty to sixty percent. Cost-to-collect is the share of every dollar billed that gets eaten by the collection process itself. The wide range is intentional. The low end is what you get when you bolt agents onto a legacy stack. The high end is what you get when you redesign the operating model around the agents. This is the most actionable healthcare private equity thesis on the desk right now. Revenue cycle specialists like R one and Waystar, plus the long tail of vertical RCM platforms, either rebuild as touchless or get repriced as legacy services over the next eighteen months.

Item four. McKinsey published a quantum readiness piece that frames quantum decryption as a present-tense risk rather than a future one. The argument is harvest-now-decrypt-later. Quick definition. Quantum computers, when they reach a certain scale, can break the cryptography that secures most of today's internet traffic and stored data. The math behind RSA and elliptic-curve encryption depends on problems that are hard for classical computers but tractable for sufficiently large quantum computers. The harvest-now-decrypt-later attack model assumes adversaries are intercepting and storing encrypted traffic today on the bet that cryptographically relevant quantum machines arrive in five to ten years. For data with long sensitivity windows, particularly patient records, intellectual property, and government archives, the breach has effectively already occurred. The framework McKinsey introduces is a Crypto-Agility Maturity Model that adapts NIST post-quantum cryptography guidance to enterprise migration sequencing. NIST is the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the US agency that sets the standards for cryptographic algorithms. They published their first post-quantum standards in 2024. For healthcare private equity specifically, this means post-quantum migration is the next CFIUS-style diligence checkbox. CFIUS is the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, the inter-agency body that reviews acquisitions for national security risk. The reference point is that data-sensitivity diligence is becoming a deal-blocking topic, not a cleanup topic. If you are underwriting a seven-year hold on a healthcare data asset, the quantum migration capex is already in your model whether you have named it or not.

Item five. McKinsey also published a case study on a non-profit merger of two veteran services organizations. The framework innovation is what they are calling the Mission-Adjusted Synergy Model, which adapts commercial mergers and acquisitions one hundred-day playbooks to mission-driven org integrations. The interesting twist is that McKinsey explicitly mapped distinct missions and program models as the equivalent of brand equity in a commercial deal. Brand equity in a commercial M and A model is the part of purchase price you pay above book value because the target's brand is itself a producing asset. McKinsey is doing the same accounting move with mission. Why this matters for portfolio construction is that any private equity roll-up with mission-driven anchor assets, which includes most healthcare services, faith-based providers, and community-anchored care models, suddenly has a vocabulary for valuing mission integrity inside the deal model rather than treating it as a sentimental veto.

Item six. McKinsey is also out with an explainer on sovereign artificial intelligence. The frame is control over compute, models, data, and deployment within national borders. Sovereign AI in plain terms means a country owning the full AI stack from chips up to applications, instead of renting capability from US or Chinese hyperscalers. The historical parallel they draw is the nineteen fifties and sixties national-champion debate in telephony and aerospace, where market efficiency and national autonomy diverged. Countries built their own national airlines and their own phone monopolies even when buying from Boeing or AT and T would have been cheaper, because the strategic capability mattered more than the unit cost. The connection to today's OpenAI IPO pressure is that index providers contorting their rules to include OpenAI and SpaceX is a form of de facto industrial policy, just routed through capital markets rather than legislation. Industrial policy is government action that deliberately shapes which industries grow, usually through subsidies, tariffs, or procurement. The thread also connects to Friday's Wall Street Journal A1 surveillance dragnet feature on the federal three hundred million-person data fabric. The same govtech and sovereignty thread runs through both.

Item seven. The Wall Street Journal has a piece on Spirit Airlines and the broader budget airline crisis driven by the Iran-conflict fuel cost spillover. Spirit is winding down. Frontier and similar carriers face the same profit and loss geometry. ULCC is the industry term for ultra-low-cost carrier. These airlines run on a model of ultra-low base fares plus aggressive ancillary fees for bags and seat selection. The model only works when fuel cost is low enough that the base fare can stay below the legacy carriers. Why this matters is that the ultra-low-cost carrier business model is structurally over for this cycle. Fuel cost passthrough breaks at a fare ceiling that ULCCs cannot exceed without losing the price advantage that defines them. The broader read is that the Iran-driven energy reset is now showing up in specific corporate capitulations, not just inflation prints. Watch which categories are next.

Item eight. The Wall Street Journal also reports that stock indexes are restructuring their rules to admit SpaceX and OpenAI ahead of formal listings. James Mackintosh's piece is about passive flow mechanics. Passive flows are the dollars that go into index funds and ETFs, which buy whatever the index says they should buy without any judgment on the individual stock. Once a private giant is index-eligible at IPO, the passive bid alone can reprice the entire cohort. For LPs sitting on AI-heavy growth fund commitments, this is a thread to track because passive demand for the listed names becomes a benchmark cap that private comparables cannot escape. LPs in this context are limited partners, the institutions like pension funds and sovereign wealth funds that put up the capital that PE and venture funds invest.

Item nine. The Wall Street Journal has a component-level analysis of how the Pentagon would try to break China's drone dominance. The piece walks through an FPV drone bill-of-materials. FPV stands for first-person view, the type of camera-equipped drone that has become tactically dominant in Ukraine. The conclusion is that industrial policy can reshore some layers, particularly battery management and final assembly, but motor magnets and certain semiconductors are bottlenecked for at least three to five years. Reshoring means relocating manufacturing back to domestic soil from offshore. For defense-tech private equity, the implication is that there is a real reshoring thesis, but the playable layers are narrower than the policy headlines suggest.

Deep dive one. The Fed regime change. Step back from the Powell departure and look at the structure. The committee held the funds rate at three point five to three point seven five percent on April twenty-ninth, with one dissent for a cut and three more dissents on the easing-bias language. That is already a dovish committee on the language vote. With Powell gone, the inflation-credibility argument loses its most institutional voice at exactly the moment that the Economist's predictive consumer-price index is reading three point six percent against the Fed's two percent target. The Economist's predictive index uses real-time commodity and retail signal to lead the official BLS print, which is the Bureau of Labor Statistics monthly inflation release. So the setup for the next meeting is a more dovish committee facing a hotter inflation tape, with the Kevin Warsh productivity-cuts intellectual case providing cover for cutting anyway. Quick context on Warsh. Kevin Warsh is a former Fed governor and is widely considered the leading external candidate to replace Powell as chair. He has argued publicly that AI-driven productivity gains lower the inflationary cost of rate cuts, because a more productive economy can absorb easier money without overheating. That is the intellectual cover phrase, the academic framing that lets the committee cut even when inflation looks high.

Watch Friday's April jobs report and the next consumer price index print. The dovish path now has fewer veto points and more political cover. For private equity underwriting, this widens the policy uncertainty band on both sides. The dovish tail, where cuts arrive faster than the curve implies, is fatter. The political-pressure tail, where the committee cuts into stagflation, is also fatter. Stagflation is the painful combination of weak growth and high inflation, which is hard for the Fed because the standard fix for one makes the other worse. Both ends are bad for credit spreads in different ways. A credit spread is the extra yield a corporate bond pays over a treasury bond of the same maturity. Spreads widen when investors demand more compensation for risk. Investment grade credit, which is the highest-rated category of corporate debt where most institutional money lives, has not yet priced this regime-change risk premium.

Deep dive two. The McKinsey Touchless Revenue Cycle thesis. Pair this with the Bain healthcare report for the full picture. The reason this is the most actionable healthcare private equity thesis right now is that revenue cycle management is the only healthcare function where cost-to-collect is a directly measurable line item on the provider's profit and loss. Every other function, including clinical efficiency and patient experience, is mediated by quality metrics or patient outcomes that are slow and noisy. RCM is the cleanest test case for whether agentic artificial intelligence actually delivers operating leverage. Operating leverage means the rate at which profit grows faster than revenue when fixed costs are spread over more output. McKinsey's thirty to sixty percent compression band is consistent with what the leading vendors are reporting. The framework to carry into any healthcare private equity conversation is this. If a target's RCM operating model still has humans on every claim, that is a screening flag, not a thesis. The platforms that win this cycle are the ones running their own engineering teams, not the ones reselling third-party artificial intelligence layered on top of legacy workflows. This is why the May first McKinsey infrastructure piece on the Rewired three-plane stack matters. Control plane, data plane, governance plane. The control plane orchestrates which agent does what when. The data plane handles where data sits and how agents access it through a semantic layer that translates raw fields into business meaning. The governance plane is the audit, cost attribution, and policy enforcement layer that lets you actually trust the agents in production. Any healthcare AI platform that cannot articulate which plane it owns and which ones it depends on is renting its margin from somebody else.

Deep dive three. The OpenAI IPO as a forcing function across the AI investment cycle. The structural insight is that the lock-up dynamics around an OpenAI listing reshape every adjacent fundraise. A lock-up is the period after an IPO during which insiders cannot sell their shares, typically ninety to one hundred eighty days. Until OpenAI prints, the last marker for AI valuation is whatever the last private round was, which has been the source of the multiple-compression argument for the last six months. Once OpenAI prints, three things happen at once. First, every Series C agentic platform raise gets benchmarked against OpenAI's revenue multiple, which will be lower than current private comps. That is multiple compression for the long tail. Second, index inclusion mechanics turn passive flows into a price floor for the listed AI cohort, which becomes a benchmark cap on private-market valuations rather than a private-market premium. Third, the platform layer becomes a public-market discipline category, which makes the picks-and-shovels layer, particularly governance, cost attribution, and model routing, more defensibly investable. McKinsey's Rewired three-plane stack and the May second Quarterly piece on where AI does not create value are the right frameworks for this. Productivity AI is now the new enterprise resource planning. Required to compete, insufficient to win. ERP, which is enterprise resource planning, was the late nineteen nineties software wave that every company had to buy from SAP or Oracle, but that did not actually create competitive advantage because everyone bought it. The durable rents are at the offering and business-model layer, not the cost-out layer. Rents in economics means returns above the normal cost of capital, the kind of profit a true moat throws off.

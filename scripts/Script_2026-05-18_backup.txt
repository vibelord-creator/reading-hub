Deep dive number one: the Powell to Warsh Fed handoff, and why the closest historical parallel is not 2018 or even 2008. It is 1979.

Jerome Powell's term as Fed Chair expires on May 15th, 2026 — Friday past. Kevin Warsh is the leading appointment. The Federal Funds effective rate is three point six three percent. Futures are pricing roughly three point seven percent late 2026 and three point eight percent in May 2027. The bond market is implying that the Fed holds, or even hikes, against an energy shock that has stalled the previous dovish pivot. April CPI ran at three point eight percent. Brent crude is above one hundred dollars. The Dallas Fed has quantified the Iran-Hormuz disruption as adding one point seven percentage points to annualized first-quarter headline CPI and zero point six percentage points to fourth-quarter-over-fourth-quarter 2026 CPI.

Here is the parallel. In 1979, G. William Miller left the Federal Reserve after a brief, undistinguished tenure during which inflation expectations became un-anchored. Paul Volcker replaced him in the middle of an energy-driven inflation shock — the second oil crisis triggered by the Iranian Revolution — with a labor market that, on the surface, looked resilient. Volcker chose to break inflation expectations through twenty percent Fed Funds rates. The pivot took two recessions and four years of nine-plus-percent unemployment. The point is not that Warsh will hike to twenty percent. The point is that this is the first Fed-chair transition under a stagflation-risk regime with an energy shock since 1979. And the institutional memory of the Burns mistake — Arthur Burns cut into the 1973 OPEC oil shock and earned a decade of inflation — is more powerful inside the Fed than any current political consideration. The macro one-oh-one inline: when you have high inflation and slow growth, the Fed is trapped. Cut to help growth and inflation re-anchors high. Hold or hike to fight inflation and you push the economy into recession. There is no clean option. Volcker chose recession. Warsh's tested instinct, per his 2010 dissent against quantitative easing, is hawkish. The market is now pricing for that.

For private equity, the knock-on effect is structural. Cost of debt remains elevated. LBO equity checks stay high. Deals trend smaller, with more minority structures or platform add-ons over new platforms. Private credit funds face more covenant defaults, which tightens new originations, particularly for clinical-services roll-ups exposed to the kind of state-level bans Vermont just advanced. Distressed healthcare opportunities accelerate in the second half. The relative winner is AI-software and tech-enabled services platforms with low leverage and fast cash conversion. That is your operating template for the next eighteen months.

Deep dive number two: why both Anthropic and OpenAI picked private equity, not hyperscalers, as their joint venture distribution channel. The framework is McKinsey's Rewired — six capabilities, three Horizons, and a clear answer on where alpha lives.

Hyperscalers are competitors. Azure and Amazon Web Services sell their own model layers and have every incentive to bundle Anthropic and OpenAI's output behind a proprietary front-end. The choice signals that frontier labs believe ops-intensive deployment, not model improvement, is the next-quarter value driver. That is a profound shift. It is the same shift McKinsey has been signaling, paper after paper, since the Rewired manifesto last April. Apply it to your career: every time you see a healthcare PE target with AI spend and flat EBITDA, that is structurally the same problem the frontier labs are solving with these joint ventures. They are missing two or more of the six Rewired capabilities. The diligence playbook is to diagnose which two, price the gap into the entry multiple, and close the gap post-close with a McKinsey-style operating model deployment.

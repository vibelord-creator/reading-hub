A brief note on macro-finance vocabulary, because two concepts came up repeatedly today and it is worth being precise about both. The first is the yield curve. The ten-year Treasury at four-point-three-seven percent is roughly twenty basis points above the two-year. That is a positively-sloped curve, which historically signals neutral to expansionary expectations, but the slope itself has flattened materially in the past quarter. A flatter curve in the presence of sticky inflation is exactly the geometry that produces the stagflation-lite framing we keep returning to. The second concept is the credit spread, specifically the gap between high-yield corporate bonds and Treasuries. That spread has crept up roughly forty basis points since March, despite equity markets being broadly stable. That divergence, equities holding while spreads widen, is one of the most reliable late-cycle indicators in fixed-income markets. It does not predict timing. It does flag that risk perception is repricing in the bond market faster than in the stock market. For your purposes, when you read about the ten-year yield, the two-year yield, and high-yield spreads in the same paragraph, the right mental model is that the bond market is the early-warning system and the stock market is the lagging indicator.

A second short note, this one on private-equity vocabulary, because three terms recurred today and the precise definitions matter for diligence work. Internal rate of return, abbreviated IRR, is the annualized return on a private-equity investment net of fees and carry. Multiple of invested capital, abbreviated MOIC, is the gross multiple before time-weighting. Public market equivalent, abbreviated PME, compares a private investment against a public-market benchmark on a cash-flow-matched basis. When Bain reports top-quartile buyout returns of eight percent versus the S&P 500 at eighteen percent, those are pooled IRRs, not MOICs and not PMEs. The PME comparison would be even less flattering for private equity in 2025, because a fully cash-flow-matched calculation accounts for the lock-up cost of having capital trapped in funds rather than free to compound in public markets. The 2025 vintage will be remembered as the year the PME gap became impossible to argue around.

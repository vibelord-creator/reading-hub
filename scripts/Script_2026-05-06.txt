The single biggest anchor today is the oil move. Brent crude fell eight percent to one hundred and one dollars a barrel after Axios reported that the White House believes the United States and Iran are close to a war-ending deal. Quick context. Brent is the global oil benchmark, the price set by North Sea crude that prices most international supply. The Hormuz commercial-ship escort operation, which the Trump administration paused this morning citing negotiation progress, was the visible signal that the Pentagon was hardening for a longer confrontation. Pausing it is a tell that the cease-fire path is real enough to act on. Equities ripped in response. The Stoxx six hundred, which is the broad European index, was up two point one percent. The FTSE one hundred in London was up two percent. S and P five hundred futures were up roughly seven-tenths of a percent into the cash open. And the ten-year Treasury yield fell six basis points to four point three five percent. A basis point is one one-hundredth of a percentage point, the standard measure for fixed-income moves. So six basis points in one session on a peace headline is meaningful but not dramatic. The ten-year is the benchmark long-term rate that mortgage and corporate borrowing costs track.

Now, a quick macro one-oh-one moment, because this matters for how you read the rest of the week. When oil drops sharply on a peace headline, the textbook reflation-pause trade plays out in three legs. First, yields fall as inflation expectations ease, because cheaper energy passes through into headline consumer-price-index prints with about a two-month lag. Second, duration outperforms, which is just bond-trader shorthand for long-dated bonds rising in price faster than short-dated ones. Third, the equity rotation favors consumer staples, airlines, and rate-sensitive sectors like utilities and homebuilders. All three legs played out this morning, which tells you the market is taking the cease-fire at face value.

Here is the wrinkle. The same morning the cease-fire broke, Australia committed ten billion Australian dollars, which is about seven point two billion U.S. dollars, to a one-billion-litre national fuel reserve. That is a fifty-day strategic stockpile. Allies do not build fifty-day fuel reserves when they believe in a clean structural reset. They build them when they believe the next disruption is coming and the only question is when. So the right way to read this morning is two-handed. The market is taking the cease-fire. Sovereigns are pricing the next one. The Iran risk premium that was probably fifteen to twenty dollars a barrel is draining out of the spot price right now, but the structural bid underneath, which is sovereign stockpiling plus the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve refill plus China teapot demand, is what determines whether ninety-five dollars holds when the headlines fade.

This is the live test of the Brent-at-one-hundred-eight thesis from May second and the Hormuz oil-risk thread that has been running since March thirtieth. The headline is reversing. The structural question, whether the war premium was the only thing keeping oil bid, has not been answered yet. For private equity underwriting on healthcare deals, this matters because every leveraged buyout sensitivity model from the last six weeks has been running an energy-passthrough scenario into wage inflation, particularly nurse-union renegotiations. If Brent round-trips into the eighties by month-end, that sensitivity becomes upside. If it stalls at ninety-five and grinds higher on the next Hormuz incident, it becomes the central case.

Now to the second regime-change print of the morning. The Securities and Exchange Commission unanimously moved a Trump-backed proposal to a sixty-day public-comment window that would let public companies file one semi-annual report instead of three quarterly tens. The new form would be called Form ten-S. Today's company files three Form ten-Q reports each year, which are the unaudited quarterly snapshots, plus one Form ten-K, the audited annual. Under the proposal, companies could opt for one semi-annual ten-S plus the annual ten-K, dropping two filings per year. The Commission chair cited rule rigidity as a drag on investment. Analysts expect finalization by year-end 2026.

This is one of the largest unforced disclosure-regime changes since Sarbanes-Oxley in 2002. Sarbanes-Oxley was the post-Enron law that mandated a cluster of new corporate disclosures and audit requirements, which created the modern compliance complex. Walking back quarterly reporting goes the other direction. For private equity, the implication cuts both ways. On one side, recurring public-comp data goes from a given to a luxury. Quarterly comp-set diligence, which deal teams take for granted as a free input, becomes meaningfully harder. On the other side, the take-private pitch has leaned for a decade on the argument that public-market short-termism, driven by the ninety-day reporting cycle, destroys long-horizon investment. If ten-Qs become optional, half that argument quietly evaporates. The audit and Sarbanes-Oxley cost differential persists, so the math on staying public actually improves before it gets worse. Take-privates do not stop, but the narrative around them shifts.

A historical aside, because this is the kind of thing McKinsey partners use to make a point in a meeting. Quarterly reporting was not part of the original 1934 Securities Exchange Act. The Securities and Exchange Commission required only annual reports through 1955, then moved to semi-annual in 1955, and only mandated quarterly disclosure in 1970. The push for quarterly came from a specific failure mode. The conglomerate-era accounting scandals at Litton and Loews Theatres-Tisch and the Penn Central railroad bankruptcy in 1970 showed that investors could not see earnings deterioration fast enough during a credit-cycle turn. Commissioner Hamer Budge pushed quarterly disclosure as the policy response. Today's Commission is essentially walking back a 1970 reform born from the exact failure mode we are flirting with again in 2026's stagflation-lite environment. The historical irony is hard to miss, and it is the kind of frame that makes the headline land differently in a Wharton classroom or an investment committee.

Item three on the regime-change list. Apollo Global Management reported first-quarter 2026 results before the open and crossed one trillion dollars in assets under management. Apollo is the third U.S. alternative-asset manager to clear the one-trillion mark, after Blackstone and Kohlberg Kravis Roberts. Total inflows were a record one hundred and fifteen billion dollars in the quarter. Origination, which is the dollar volume of new private-credit loans Apollo wrote, was seventy-one billion. Capital Solutions fees set an all-time high at two hundred and forty-six million. Marc Rowan, the chief executive, framed the print as evidence of the value Apollo creates at scale. Wall Street is parsing the private-credit margin trajectory closely, but the headline is the AUM milestone.

Here is why the origination number matters more than the AUM milestone for what we care about. Assets under management is largely a backward-looking measure. It captures committed capital from prior fundraises. Origination measures flow, the rate at which private credit is replacing syndicated leveraged-loan and high-yield bond issuance in real time. Seventy-one billion dollars of origination in a single quarter is, on an annualized basis, larger than the entire 2009 syndicated-loan market. Private credit is no longer a complement to public credit. It is the mid-market.

For healthcare private equity specifically, this is the cleanest read on whether the rate-cut path Kevin Warsh has been intellectualizing actually materializes. If credit spreads compress as the Federal Reserve pivots, origination accelerates further, fee-related earnings inflate, and Apollo's margins expand. If stagflation-lite holds, origination slows but defaults rise, which compresses margins on the loans already on the books. The trade-through for sponsor-backed healthcare deals is direct. Lower spreads on private credit mean cheaper LBO debt, mean higher purchase prices on the platform companies the sponsors are bidding for. So Apollo's print today is the leading indicator on whether the back half of 2026 sees deal volume re-accelerate.

That is the regime-change top of the show. Now to the rest of the sweep.

The McKinsey research team published three pieces this week that, taken together, are the most useful AI-investment frame anyone has put on paper. The first is the QuantumBlack practice's Five-Layer AI Measurement Stack. The argument is that most companies can prove model accuracy at Layer One but cannot trace a line from accuracy to earnings at Layer Five. The five layers in order are model performance, adoption and engagement, operational impact, strategic outcomes, and financial performance. The failure point in eighty percent of enterprise AI deployments is not the model. It is Layer Two, adoption. The framework gives the control plane, which we discussed Monday in the context of the Rewired three-plane stack, its actual key performance indicators. McKinsey is also prescribing a governance cadence: monthly operational reviews and quarterly strategic reviews, anchored to a single shared evidence pack covering benefits, total cost of ownership, adoption, and technical health. For Wharton coursework and private-equity diligence, this is the most useful read of the week. It gives you a defensible answer to "how do we underwrite AI value capture?" that does not depend on management-team optimism.

The second piece is from the Strategy and Corporate Finance practice, titled "Where AI will create value, and where it won't." The thesis is that AI value capture is bimodal. Workflow re-design generates durable returns because the redesigned workflow itself is hard to replicate. Cost-out efficiency plays generate transient returns because every competitor gets the same vendor stack inside eighteen months. The implication is that the productivity-AI narrative dominating earnings calls right now is the wrong scoreboard. McKinsey applies its Three Horizons framework to AI here. Three Horizons is a Geoffrey Moore-influenced strategy framework where Horizon One is the existing core business, Horizon Two is emerging adjacencies, and Horizon Three is options on the future. Applied to AI: Horizon One is productivity, which is table stakes. Horizon Two is workflow reinvention, which is durable margin. Horizon Three is business-model rewriting, which is where alpha actually compounds. The companies winning in 2028 are not the ones cutting headcount on AI productivity gains. They are the ones reinvesting cost savings into Horizon-Two workflow redesign.

The third McKinsey piece is the Rewired second edition, an update of the 2023 transformation playbook. The original six-capability model holds, but agentic AI compresses two of the layers, data and talent, and amplifies the governance layer. The new emphasis is what McKinsey calls AI debt. Eighty percent of enterprises are bolting agents onto legacy stacks rather than redesigning. The shortcut feels fast today. It compounds the way technical debt did between 2014 and 2018, where the payment came due in the form of thirty-million-dollar re-platforming charges. AI debt is going to be the diligence vocabulary of 2027. Today's add-an-agent-to-Salesforce shortcut is tomorrow's mandatory rebuild.

These three pieces are not independent. They form a vocabulary ladder. Three Horizons tells you which AI investments are durable. Five-Layer Measurement tells you how to underwrite each one. AI Debt tells you what happens to the ones that look durable on paper but skip Layer Two adoption. By the third quarter, this vocabulary will be in investment-committee memos.

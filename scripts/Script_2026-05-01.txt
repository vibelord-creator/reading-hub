Deep dive two. Off-balance-sheet leverage in private credit, and why this matters for healthcare PE. Let us walk through the mechanics, because this is a finance basic worth knowing cold. A BDC reports a stated leverage ratio — call it one-point-five times debt-to-equity. That is what shows up in the regulatory filing. Now imagine the BDC sets up a wholly-owned SPV — a subsidiary that does not consolidate fully on the parent's balance sheet. The SPV borrows separately, often with a repo line or warehouse facility, and uses that borrowing to buy more loan assets. The economic exposure is identical to leverage on the parent — the parent owns the SPV, owns the assets, owns the risk — but the stated leverage ratio at the parent does not move. Now layer in joint ventures with another credit fund — same trick, off-balance-sheet, often with non-recourse structuring. Weil's claim is that for many large publicly listed BDCs, the economic leverage is a full turn or more above the stated leverage. What does this mean for healthcare LBO underwriting? When you finance a healthcare buyout with unitranche debt from one of these BDCs, you are not just underwriting your portco's free cash flow. You are also implicitly underwriting the BDC's JV partner. If a downturn breaks the JV partner, the BDC's effective cost of funding spikes, which cascades back into your portco's refinancing risk three or four years out. This is the same chain of logic that took down Lehman in two thousand eight, when repo-one-oh-five hid balance-sheet leverage right up until it could not. Cross-temporal connection: this is the risk-side complement to the April twenty-sixth Key Themes entry on PE returns being driven by operational alpha. If reported leverage understates economic leverage, top-quartile alpha must clear an even higher economic cost-of-capital hurdle than the reported figures suggest. Operational alpha gets harder when the leverage you cannot see is real.

Deep dive three. The agentic AI control-plane thesis, and why it matters more than the model wars. The McKinsey infrastructure piece does something subtle. It reframes the agentic AI conversation away from model quality — GPT-five-point-five versus Claude Opus four-point-seven, which lab is winning — and toward the orchestration layer. The argument: eighty percent of enterprises are accumulating governance debt by bolting agents onto legacy systems. Each agent has its own identity, its own permissions, its own observability — or, more often, none of those things. That works at five agents in production. It does not work at five hundred. By twenty twenty-seven, every enterprise that took agentic AI seriously in twenty twenty-five and twenty twenty-six will need a control plane: a single layer that handles agent identity, permissions, model routing, observability, and cost attribution. That control plane is not the model. It is not the agent itself. It is the connective tissue. Cross-temporal connection: this is what the April fifteenth Key Themes entry — ninety-four percent of enterprises flagging governance sprawl — was predicting. The same pattern played out in the cloud era. The cloud winners were not the operating system vendors. They were the AWSes and the Snowflakes — the orchestration and data layers. The agent winners will not be the foundation-model labs. They will be the control-plane vendors. The investment implication is to track who is selling agent control planes to large enterprises in twenty twenty-six, because they will be the next decade's Snowflake.

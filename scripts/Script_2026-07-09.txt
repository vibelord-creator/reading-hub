Now the deep dives. A few stories that reward slowing down.

First deep dive: the historical parallel underneath the Chinese-models story, because this is where you get to sound like you've thought about it for longer than a news cycle. Inference commoditization rhymes with the telecom bandwidth collapse of the early 2000s. Here's the analogy. In the late nineties, an enormous overbuild of long-haul fiber crushed the price of moving a bit of data across the country. Companies like Global Crossing and WorldCom laid far more capacity than anyone could use, and per-bit pricing collapsed toward zero. If you owned the fiber, that was a catastrophe; several of those companies went bankrupt. But the value didn't vanish. It migrated up the stack, to the businesses that rode all that suddenly-cheap bandwidth: Google, Amazon, Netflix. Streaming video, cloud computing, and search were only economical because the thing underneath them had been driven to near-free. Now map that onto today. As tokens get cheap, and a Chinese open-weight model matching Anthropic at a fifth of the cost is exactly that force in action, durable margin accrues not to the raw model but to the orchestration, governance, and workflow layer on top. The investable question isn't "who has the best model." It's "who captures the surplus when the model is nearly free." In the last cycle, that was the application layer. This time it plausibly is too. The frameworks connect directly: McKinsey's governance-gap piece is quantifying, in real numbers, exactly where that up-stack value is accumulating.

Second deep dive: the insider-led take-private structure at Select Medical, because the governance question here is a good interview-grade one. When management rolls its own equity into a take-private alongside the sponsor, you get a genuine double-edged sword. On the good edge, alignment: the operators now own a real slice of the outcome, and their willingness to roll equity rather than cash out is an inside signal of confidence in the forward plan. Limited partners generally love that. On the sharp edge, concentration and conflict. Management is now sitting on both sides of the negotiating table. They're buyers of a company they also run, which means they have superior information about its true value and an incentive to set the take-private price low. That's precisely why these deals draw shareholder scrutiny and litigation over the fairness of the board process and the independence of the special committee. So when you see "management-led buyout," the reflex should be two questions: how much equity did management actually roll, and how independent was the process that set the price? The answer to the first tells you about alignment; the answer to the second tells you about the risk that minority holders got shortchanged.

Third deep dive: connect the Fed and the deal flow, because this is the mechanism that ties your macro reading to your career thesis, and it's worth being able to explain cold. Why would a Fed hike cool healthcare PE? Two channels. First, the cost of debt. Leveraged buyouts are, by definition, financed heavily with borrowed money. When the policy rate rises, the interest on that debt rises, which directly reduces the equity returns a sponsor can earn on a leveraged deal. Second, the discount rate. The value of any asset is the present value of its future cash flows, and you discount those future flows back at a rate anchored to the risk-free curve. A higher curve means you divide future cash flows by a bigger number, which mechanically lowers what any asset is worth today, compressing entry multiples across the board. Put those together and a July hike would slow the large-cap, heavily-levered end of the market and push sponsors toward all-equity or asset-light, tech-enabled targets, exactly the kind of revenue-cycle software business in that Experity-Exdion deal, where returns depend on operational improvement rather than financial leverage. Notice how the whole newsletter rhymes: the same rate environment that threatens the classic LBO is the one steering capital toward the tech-enabled healthcare assets you care about.

Fourth deep dive, and this one resolves an apparent paradox from the catch-up pile. Last week's June jobs report missed badly: fifty-seven thousand jobs added against an estimate of a hundred and fifteen thousand, roughly half of what economists expected. And yet stocks rose, and yet the Fed's own minutes now show nine officials leaning toward a hike. Stop and notice how strange that looks on the surface. A weak labor market is normally the exact input that pushes a central bank to cut, not hike. So what's going on? Two things worth understanding, because this is precisely the kind of macro puzzle you're actively working to be able to reason through. First, the market's logic. A soft jobs print, in a world where the Fed has stopped easing on autopilot, gets read as "the economy is cooling just enough to keep inflation from reaccelerating, without actually breaking." That's the fabled soft landing, and equities like it. Bad news for the labor market becomes good news for stocks, as long as it's mild bad news rather than the start of a genuine downturn. Second, the Fed's logic, and this is the more important half. The reason those minutes still carry a hawkish bias despite the weak jobs number is that the committee is weighing two risks against each other, and right now it judges the inflation risk to be the more dangerous one. A hike-dot doesn't mean an official thinks the economy is strong. It means they think the cost of letting inflation re-accelerate is higher than the cost of a slightly softer labor market. That's a judgment about which mistake is more expensive to unwind, and it's shaped by institutional memory of the nineteen-seventies, when the Fed cut too early, inflation came roaring back, and it took a punishing recession to finally kill it. So the honest synthesis of the week is this: a weak jobs report did not move the Fed dovish, because in the current mental model, the labor market cooling is the intended result and inflation is the thing that still keeps them up at night. When you can hold both of those logics in your head at once, you understand the regime.

If durable value is systematically migrating one layer down from the obvious asset, then the best risk-adjusted returns of this cycle sit with the businesses that own that lower layer, the picks-and-shovels underneath the gold rush. In healthcare, that's the revenue-cycle software, the data infrastructure, the specialized services that get more valuable precisely as the flashy thing above them commoditizes. When you're evaluating a target, the sharpest single question you can ask is: is this business the asset everyone's watching, or is it the layer underneath that captures the surplus when the watched asset gets cheap? The second kind is where this market is rewarding capital.

And one honest caveat, because the point of this reading habit is to think, not just to nod along. The "value migrates down a layer" thesis is elegant, and elegance is exactly when you should be suspicious. The counter-case is real: sometimes the frontier asset keeps its moat because switching costs, brand, or regulatory capture protect it, and the commoditization never fully arrives. Nvidia held its pricing power far longer than the "chips will commoditize" crowd predicted. So hold the framework as a strong prior, not a law. The discipline is to ask, for each specific asset, what would actually stop the value from migrating, and whether that thing is present here. If you can't name a durable reason the top layer keeps its margin, the thesis holds. If you can, it doesn't.

The single biggest story this week is not a deal. It is a number. The thirty-year Treasury yield punched through five point one nine percent on Tuesday, the highest print since July 2007, which is to say the highest level we have seen since the months immediately before the global financial crisis. The Bank of America fund manager survey, taken in the same window, showed that sixty-two percent of global allocators now expect the long bond to reach six percent. That sixty-two percent reading is the highest since late 1999. Read those two numbers together. The market is no longer pricing a soft landing. It is pricing a regime change.

Here is the McKinsey-style so-what. The thirty-year yield is real rates plus expected inflation plus the term premium. When all three of those drift up at the same time, it is not a cyclical move, it is a re-anchoring. For private equity and venture, that re-anchoring is the underwriting environment for the next two vintages. Discount rates are not going back to 2021 levels. The 2021 vintage, in particular, is the most exposed: it was paper underwritten to two percent inflation and falling rates, and the maturity wall hits hardest in 2026 and 2027 for unitranche and covenant-light healthcare and consumer LBOs.

Now hold that thought and stack it against what Ray Dalio said on Monday and what Apollo's Torsten Slok has been saying for weeks. Dalio called the current regime, in his words, a stagflationary period, and warned explicitly that cutting rates under the new Fed chairmanship would damage Fed credibility. Slok frames it the same way: tariffs, sticky inflation, and slowing growth equals stagflation-lite, with the consensus first cut now pushed back to the fourth quarter at the earliest. The political pressure on the Fed is still pointed in the opposite direction.

That is the trade. The political ask is to cut. The data, and Dalio, and the bond market are all saying that the chair who cuts here will own the inflation re-acceleration that follows. And here is the historical parallel that anchors the whole frame. May 2026 looks more like August 1979 than any post-financial-crisis transition we have lived through. In August of 1979, G. William Miller handed the Fed over to Paul Volcker. Oil was rallying on the Iranian Revolution. CPI was accelerating. The outgoing chair was perceived as politically captured. Volcker chose credibility over growth, broke inflation by 1983, and paid for it with the 1980 recession and the much deeper 1981 to 1982 recession. The Powell-to-Warsh handoff has the same shape. The question for Kevin Warsh, once confirmed, is whether he wants to be the next Volcker or whether he wants to be remembered as another Miller.

For the EMBA bench, here is a one-minute macro primer that is worth keeping in your back pocket. Stagflation means stagnant growth plus persistent inflation. Cutting rates in that regime normally stimulates demand. But in a stagflation regime, cutting rates un-anchors inflation expectations. The central bank loses its anti-inflation premium, the long end of the yield curve re-steepens, and duration-heavy portfolios get repriced down. That is the path Dalio is warning against, and it is the path the bond market is pricing.

Now let me do a deep dive on the McKinsey Primary Care, Reimagined piece, because it is the demand-side anchor for almost every healthcare PE thesis getting done right now. The numbers, in order. Forty-five million Americans have unmet primary care needs today. The unmet demand gap is one hundred and seventy million visits. The United States spends roughly five percent of total health spend on primary care, against an OECD average of about thirteen percent. Three condition cohorts, Alzheimer's, behavioral health, and metabolic disease, account for seventy percent of all primary care visits and three times the average visit count per patient.

McKinsey then models three intervention scenarios. Workforce revitalization, mostly nurse-practitioner scope expansion and AI-enabled productivity, could add ninety-six million visits of capacity by 2040. Specialty innovation, including scaling GLP-1s to twenty-five percent of obese adults, could save approximately thirty-four billion dollars per year in inpatient and emergency department costs by 2050, but, and this is the critical sentence, it would add roughly ninety million additional annual primary care visits for ongoing monitoring. Integrated behavioral health care at fifty percent adoption could save another fifty-seven billion by 2050. Population health improvement to Scandinavian benchmarks would add one hundred and fifteen million visits of capacity.

The framework here is the Workforce Capacity Gap Model. Supply equals clinicians times productivity. Demand equals condition cohorts times visits per patient. The partner talking point that lands is this: scaling GLP-1s to a quarter of obese adults pays for itself by saving thirty-four billion dollars a year in inpatient costs, but only if primary care capacity expands to absorb the ninety million additional annual visits. That is the GLP-1 second-order effect that almost nobody is underwriting. The drug saves money downstream. It consumes the binding constraint upstream. The HST plays that expand primary care throughput, AI scribing, RCM automation, NP scope expansion, asynchronous care, are leveraged plays on GLP-1 economics, and that is the thesis backing every meaningful HST PE deal getting done in 2026.

Stack that against McKinsey's other healthcare AI piece from this week, which estimates that AI, automation, and care-model redesign could unlock nine to fifteen percent of US national health expenditure, roughly four hundred to seven hundred billion dollars. For providers, automating prior authorization, clinical documentation, and coding could boost gross revenue eleven to seventeen percent. For payers, AI care management could cut medical costs five to eleven percent, and administrative AI could save thirteen to twenty-five percent. Sixty-one percent of healthcare organizations deploying generative AI are doing so via third-party vendor partnerships rather than building. That last data point is the underwriting tell. The vendors that can stand up agentic workflows for prior auth, charge capture, denials management, and clinical documentation are sitting on an addressable market that McKinsey has now sized at hundreds of billions.

The diligence framework McKinsey recommends is Three Horizons. Horizon one is administrative automation, capturable now. Horizon two is clinical decision support, eighteen to thirty-six months out. Horizon three is integrated AI-native care delivery, three to seven years. Most healthcare organizations are over-indexed on horizon one quick wins, and most sponsors over-pay for horizon one because it is the most quantifiable. The actual alpha lives in horizon two. The modal diligence mistake of 2026 is mispricing the horizon one to horizon two split. That sentence is worth keeping.

On the McKinsey geopolitics piece, the headline number is fourteen trillion dollars of trade value at stake, equal to thirty-one percent of projected 2035 trade. The five plays the report recommends are: prioritize trade corridors, deploy capital into resilient locations, strengthen operational resilience, build organizational agility with geopolitical exposure scoring, and manage near-term earnings exposure. The framework McKinsey names is the Trade Corridor Resilience Matrix: safe bets are India-linked, cautious bets are ASEAN-US and EU-Latin America, uncertain bets are China- and Russia-linked. The most damning sentence in the entire piece is that only forty-two percent of supply chain executives understand suppliers below tier one, and that number is down from 2022. The supplier visibility problem has gotten worse, not better. Healthcare has a direct analogue: over sixty percent of active pharmaceutical ingredients are sourced from India or China.

The partner talking point is straightforward. McKinsey says fourteen trillion dollars of trade is genuinely contested, a third of 2035 trade, and the CEOs winning right now treat geopolitics as a P and L line, not a risk-management appendix. That framing is also, by the way, the structural backbone of why the long bond is selling off. The deglobalization premium is structural, not transitory. Tie that back to the thirty-year yield at five point one nine, and the bond market is essentially pricing the same risk McKinsey is documenting.

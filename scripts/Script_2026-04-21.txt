That is the news sweep. Let me now go deeper on the three stories that most shape this week's decisions.

Deep dive one: why the GVaS formula matters more than the framework. McKinsey has been writing around GVaS since April fourteenth, but this week's Quarterly is the first to publish the full formula — revenue times geopolitical distance times severity — with a specific scoring methodology. Geopolitical distance is scored zero to ten based on UN voting alignment divergence from the home jurisdiction. Severity is scored zero to one, where one means active export controls or investment restrictions and zero means stabilizing trade agreements. The tolerance curves concept is the main actionable output: asset-light functions like sales and digital services tolerate higher geopolitical distance than asset-heavy functions like manufacturing and specialized talent. Apply this to a healthcare PE portfolio. A US CDMO platform with diversified customer geographies scores low on distance and low on severity — low GVaS. A specialty-pharma company with Chinese API suppliers, EU customers, and US reimbursement scores high on distance and high on severity — high GVaS. The framework forces a conversation PE funds are currently not having: which portfolio companies would we exit today if the severity score on China moved from point seven to point nine. That conversation is worth running on every 2019 through 2021 vintage asset. It is also the single most accretive McKinsey-language item to drop into a Q2 diligence memo, because it shows partners you are operating at the framework's leading edge, not the trailing edge.

Deep dive two: the Medicare GLP-1 go-live and the cascade through commercial list pricing. The mechanics are cleaner than the headlines suggest. Medicare Part D pays two forty-five per month to Lilly and Novo for injectable GLP-1s, with beneficiary copay capped at fifty dollars. The July 2026 bridge program runs until the full BALANCE Model kicks in January 2027. TrumpRx's DTC channel opens at three fifty with a two-year glidepath to two fifty. Now layer on what happens in commercial PBM negotiations. UnitedHealth, Cigna, Elevance — all three run their commercial rebate negotiations with Medicare benchmarks and DTC list prices as reference points. Two anchors at roughly two fifty mean commercial list of six hundred plus is no longer defensible without a rebate offset. Expect twenty to thirty-five percent commercial ASP reset by 2028. This is not a pricing risk scenario — it is the base case. The McKinsey framework to apply here is the Value Creation Framework — specifically, which companies have pricing power to keep the volume benefit despite the price compression, versus which will give the margin back. For Lilly, the answer is volume leverage: an oral Foundayo pipeline and dominant share mean total units more than offset ASP decline. For Novo, the picture is different — Novo's 2026 outlook already guides for a sales decline, and reset economics accelerate that. For mid-tier competitors like Viking and Structure, the oral GLP-1 targets become more valuable on the demand side — reset economics still beat no product — and more threatened on pricing. The specialty-pharmacy and compounded-semaglutide segment is about to compress violently. That is the cleanest short-thesis setup in healthcare PE for 2026.

If you built your 2026 fund base case on three cuts, your exit multiples are now structurally wrong. A quick primer inline, because this comes up often. The Fed funds rate is the short rate. The Fed's terminal rate expectation, which feeds the dot plot, is what the committee thinks the rate will be at year-end. When JPM forecasts a hike in Q3 2027, they are saying the neutral rate is higher than the committee thinks — meaning current policy is actually less restrictive than three seventy-five implies, which is actually the hawkish mirror-image of the Warsh AI-productivity thesis.

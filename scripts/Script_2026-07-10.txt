Now the deep dives. Three stories that reward slowing down.

First deep dive, the oil paradox, because if you can explain this cold you will sound like you actually understand how commodity markets price risk. The United States hit ninety targets in Iran, Iran threatened American bases, the oil license was revoked, and crude fell two percent. Why? Because an oil price is not a measure of how scary the news is. It is a probability-weighted bet on future physical supply. And the market has now watched this exact movie several times this spring: escalation, threats, a few tense days, then tankers keep moving through the Strait of Hormuz and nothing actually gets cut off. Recall that roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil transits that strait, so a genuine closure would be a true supply shock. But the traders' revealed judgment is that we are in a new normal, periods of conflict punctuating periods of calm that still permit transit, with both sides judged to have too much to lose from destroying the region's energy infrastructure. So when fresh strikes hit an already-braced market that had priced in a war premium, and the strait still stays open, the rational move is to unwind some of that premium. The price falls on the news precisely because the feared outcome, a closed strait, did not materialize. The lesson generalizes: in any market that has been trading a risk premium for weeks, the direction of the price on a scary headline tells you less about the headline and more about how much fear was already in the price. This is the same muscle as bad-news-is-good-news in equities. The reaction is about the gap between the news and what was already discounted, not about the news itself. And notice the through-line to the Fed: as long as Hormuz stays open, oil stays contained, and contained oil is exactly what lets a split Fed even entertain patience instead of being forced to hike on an energy-driven inflation spike. The geopolitics thread and the rates thread are, once again, the same thread wearing two hats.

Second deep dive, the Peak Rock and Asembia deal, because this is where a week of reading pays a concrete dividend. Yesterday we did the GLP-1 value-migration argument: as a giant public payer covers a drug category for the first time, volume explodes, per-unit price compresses, and durable margin migrates off the molecule and downstream toward whoever controls distribution. The historical template was Medicare Part D in 2006, where the drugs got commoditized and the pharmacy benefit managers, the middle layer, got rich. Now look at what Peak Rock just bought. Asembia is not a drug. It is the distribution, the data, the hub services, the software, and the group-purchasing rails that sit underneath the specialty-drug boom, including GLP-1s. So this is not just another healthcare buyout. It is a sponsor placing a chip on exactly the square the Part D parallel tells you to bet on. When the molecule commoditizes, own the rails the molecule rides on. And here is the partner-level observation: the same structural logic that governs AI, value fleeing the commoditizing layer for the layer that controls deployment, governs specialty pharma too. In AI, the rails are orchestration and governance. In specialty drugs, the rails are distribution and data. Different industries, identical geometry. When you can see that a specialty-pharma platform deal and an AI super-app launch are the same move in different clothing, you are reading the market the way a partner reads it, structurally rather than headline by headline.

Third deep dive, the frontier labs moving into services, because there is a genuine strategic tension here worth being able to argue on both sides. The bull case for OpenAI and Anthropic building deployment arms is straightforward. If two-thirds of enterprises lack the governance maturity to run agents themselves, and McKinsey's own numbers say exactly that, then the party who supplies the deployment, the forward-deployed engineers, the guardrails, the integration, captures the recurring revenue that the raw model, racing toward commodity pricing against cheap open-weight Chinese and Nvidia models, is losing. Vertical integration into the high-margin layer is a rational response to commoditization at the low-margin layer. That is the bull case, and it is a good one. Now the bear case, and this is where you earn your seat at the table by naming the tension. Consulting and deployment services are a fundamentally different business than model research. They scale with headcount, not with compute. Margins are lower, delivery is lumpy, and you inherit the messy, human, integration-heavy work that pure software companies spend their lives trying to escape. There is a real risk that a frontier lab dilutes a pristine, high-multiple research franchise by bolting a labor-intensive services business onto it, the way more than one great product company has stumbled by chasing services revenue down-market. So which is it, moat extension or margin dilution? The honest answer is that it depends on whether the deployment layer stays genuinely hard. If governing agents remains difficult enough that it commands software-like pricing, the labs are brilliant to own it. If tooling matures and governance becomes a checkbox, they have taken on a low-margin headache. Hold both cases. The tell to watch is gross margin: if the deployment businesses report software-like margins a year from now, the bulls were right; if they look like consulting, the bears were.

Before the action items, let me pull the edition into one investing frame, because this is where the day resolves into a usable idea. Across the OpenAI and Nvidia stories, the Asembia deal, and even the GLP-1 economics, there is exactly one recurring structural claim: value is decoupling from the asset everyone watches and re-attaching to the adjacent layer that captures the surplus when that asset commoditizes. The frontier model commoditizes, so value moves to deployment and governance, and the labs are buying it. For your career thesis specifically, it means the best risk-adjusted returns this cycle likely sit with the picks-and-shovels businesses one layer off the obvious asset, the RCM software, the specialty-pharma rails, the AI governance and orchestration layer, that get more valuable precisely as the flashy thing above them gets cheap.

And the honest caveat, because the point of this habit is to think, not to nod along. That thesis is elegant, and elegance is exactly when to be suspicious. The counter-case showed up in today's own reading. When OpenAI moves into deployment services, it is betting the layer underneath stays hard and high-margin. But if that layer turns out to be commoditizable too, if governance tooling matures into a checkbox, then the value does not rest there either, it just keeps sliding, and the sponsor who paid up for the rails or the lab that built the services arm is holding a lower-margin business than they underwrote. So hold the one-layer-down thesis as a strong prior, not a law. For every asset, ask what specifically stops the value from migrating one more layer, and whether that thing is actually present. If you cannot name a durable reason the layer keeps its margin, the thesis may already be one layer stale.

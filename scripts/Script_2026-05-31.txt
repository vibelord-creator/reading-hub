Quick mechanics, because we have flagged this thread several times in the last two weeks. Core PCE is the Fed's preferred inflation gauge. It strips food and energy out of the broader personal consumption expenditures index, which is why a three percent core print is more alarming than a three percent headline print. Food and energy are the volatile pieces. When the core, the sticky part, is moving in the wrong direction, the Fed cannot tell itself a transitory story. And four dissents on a twelve-person committee is not just a vote count. It is a public signal that the institution is no longer speaking with one voice. The historical consequence of that, every time, is a wider term premium on long Treasuries. Bondholders demand more yield to compensate for the policy uncertainty. That is how Fed disunity gets priced.

Item seven. McKinsey's US healthcare M&A piece this week. The shift documented is from scope deals, which is vertical or horizontal expansion, to capability deals, which is acquiring AI-enabled diagnostics, imaging, and digital workflows. That maps onto the McKinsey M&A archetypes framework, scale, scope, capability. The implication that nobody is talking about, capability deals require preserving the target's people and culture, the opposite of cost-synergy integration. That should make the front cover of any deal book in 2026, and it does not.

Now into two deep dives, with historical context and the framework that matters.

Deep dive one. The Warsh inheritance. The instinct in the press has been to draw a Volcker parallel. Warsh takes the chair with inflation embedded, an external shock, and a credibility problem. The parallel is instructive but imperfect, and the differences matter more than the similarities. Volcker in 1979 inherited a Fed that was institutionally weak but internally unified. The famous Saturday Night Special in October 1979, when he shocked rates to twenty percent, was possible because Volcker had the room to act unilaterally and a board that, even reluctantly, fell in line. Warsh does not have that. Warsh inherits four publicly dissenting governors on his very first meeting. The Volcker move was a credibility shock by an aligned committee. The Warsh challenge is a credibility shock by a fractured committee, and there is no historical playbook for that. The honest read, the next two FOMC meetings are not going to be about rate level. They are going to be about whether Warsh can manufacture committee discipline before the bond market prices in a permanent term premium. If he cannot, the cost of capital for every private equity sponsor in the country goes up, regardless of what the funds rate actually is. That is the transmission mechanism that should be on every healthcare PE investment committee agenda this June.

Quick mechanics on the term premium, because we have used the phrase three times in two weeks. The term premium is the extra yield long Treasury bonds pay above what you would get by rolling short Treasuries. When the Fed is credible and predictable, the term premium is low, sometimes near zero. When the Fed is unpredictable, or when the institution itself looks fragile, the term premium widens because investors need to be paid for that uncertainty. A wider term premium pushes up the ten-year yield, which is the reference rate for almost every long-duration asset, including levered buyout debt. That is the channel through which Fed disunity becomes a private equity cost-of-capital problem.

Deep dive two. The PE maturation argument is now coming from two independent McKinsey and Bain sources in the same week, which is the moment to take it seriously. The frame I would use is the granularity of growth framework, McKinsey's own, which says that growth at the company or industry level decomposes into market growth, share gain, and M&A. Apply that to PE returns and you get a similar decomposition, multiple expansion, EBITDA growth, and leverage. The structural concern with the 2026 setup is that all three legs are weakening simultaneously. Multiple expansion is structurally capped because entry multiples are at eleven point eight times EBITDA. EBITDA growth is harder when the macro is stagflationary. And the cost of leverage is going up with the term premium we just discussed. That triple-squeeze is what is forcing the industry into continuation vehicles and structured secondaries. The honest critique. Continuation vehicles are not value creation. They are liquidity solutions for funds that ran out of time. They preserve fee streams for GPs and defer the moment of truth for LPs. If sixty percent of 2026 healthcare PE exits end up being sponsor-to-sponsor or continuation vehicles, the industry is not creating durable value. It is restructuring its way out of a vintage problem. That is the argument worth being able to make in a PE interview this fall.

Now the cross-temporal threads. Three are worth tracking explicitly.

Thread one. The GLP-1 pricing collapse. Three weeks ago, Lilly launched Foundayo at one hundred forty-nine dollars a month, which set the new oral price anchor. Four days ago, the Trump administration, Lilly, and Novo announced the Medicare-Medicaid pilot covering obesity drugs at fifty dollars for seniors. Today, Novo cut Wegovy and Ozempic list price by fifty percent. That is one consistent direction across three policy and corporate touchpoints in fifteen days. The pricing floor for GLP-1s is collapsing from two sides at once, payer policy from the federal level and generic threat from below. Every weight-management clinic thesis, every telehealth GLP-1 play, every compounder, needs to be re-underwritten against a fifty-dollar senior copay anchor and a generic timeline that is shorter than anyone modeled in April.

Thread two. The Fed credibility arc. The BLS commissioner was fired mid-revision cycle in late April, which raised data credibility concerns. Last Tuesday, Governor Cook publicly entertained a hike while four colleagues were already dissenting toward easier. This week, the May minutes confirmed four dissents and a hawkish tilt. And now Warsh takes the chair. That is data credibility plus decision credibility, both eroding in the same six-week window. The bond market has not fully repriced this yet. If you are tracking one thing for the June sixteen and seventeen FOMC, it is whether Warsh's first press conference attempts to restore unity language or whether he leans into the dissent.

Thread three. The agentic AI debate is now playing out across at least four verticals. Wall Street, with Anthropic and OpenAI. Healthcare revenue cycle, with McKinsey's touchless RCM modeling, thirty to sixty percent cost-to-collect reduction. CFO offices, with the PwC-OpenAI deployment. And portfolio-company back-office, which is where PE sponsors are about to compete on AI-deployment muscle. The architectural question is the same in each, model-led general agents versus vendor-built vertical agents. The honest read, neither approach has won yet, and any consultant or partner who claims otherwise is selling something. What matters for a sponsor is whether the firm has a deployment capability, not which architecture it picks.

A note on the McKinsey claim that AI is now the granular asset-quality lens for PE. That deserves a critique. McKinsey has been saying this in every report for two years. The realized adoption data does not yet support the claim. The same firm's own Europe consumer industries piece from May thirteenth found EBITDA flat in companies that increased AI spend significantly. There is a gap between the narrative McKinsey is selling about AI as a value-creation lens and the data McKinsey itself is publishing about realized AI returns. Hold that gap as a flag, not a settled conclusion.

A brief detour to a McKinsey piece worth reading in full, the touchless revenue cycle report. McKinsey models that agentic AI in provider revenue cycle could cut cost-to-collect by thirty to sixty percent. The framework is the operations transformation framework, process digitization, then exception management, then autonomous workflow. The reason this matters is that RCM is where the AI healthcare thesis becomes spreadsheet-real. A thirty to sixty percent cost-to-collect reduction is a discrete, underwritable EBITDA lever a PE sponsor can model line by line. It connects directly to the Bain finding that healthcare IT was the fastest-growing healthcare PE sub-sector last year. The critique to keep in mind, the thirty to sixty percent range is a top-quartile case study, not a portfolio average. McKinsey's own published adoption data suggests realized first-twenty-four-month results land closer to fifteen to twenty percent once change-management drag is priced in. The number you actually want in your underwriting model is twenty percent, not forty-five.

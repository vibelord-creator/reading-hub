Here's the single most important idea today. We have quietly walked back into a bad-news-is-good-news market. June payrolls came in at fifty-seven thousand jobs, roughly half of what economists expected, and stocks went up. The S&P 500 closed essentially flat at seven thousand four hundred and eighty-three on July second, with the Dow at a record, ahead of the holiday. A weak jobs report should, in a normal world, spook equity investors worried about a slowing economy. Instead it rallied them, because a soft labor market lowers the odds that the Federal Reserve hikes rates again at its July twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth meeting. When the market starts treating bad economic news as good market news, that tells you something structural: the Fed's reaction function has become the whole game. The data matters only insofar as it moves the central bank. Hold that thought, because almost everything else today connects back to it.

Let's do the news sweep.

First, the jobs number itself, because it's subtler than the headline. The economy added just fifty-seven thousand jobs in June against a consensus near one hundred and fifteen thousand. And yet the unemployment rate fell, to four point two percent from four point three. How can hiring slow and unemployment drop at the same time? Because the unemployment rate is a ratio, and the denominator, the labor force, can shrink. If enough people stop looking for work, they're no longer counted as unemployed, and the rate can fall even as job creation weakens. So a genuinely soft report and a lower unemployment rate can coexist. The lesson: watch labor force participation, not just the headline rate. This is the swing variable for the entire rate path right now, and therefore for the cost of financing leveraged buyouts and for how anyone underwrites a healthcare deal.

Second, the Fed backdrop. The FOMC has held its target range at three and a half to three and three-quarters percent for four straight meetings, under new Chair Kevin Warsh, who has run a deliberately guidance-light shop. Odds into late July are roughly sixty-forty hold versus hike, with energy-driven core inflation still sitting above target, which keeps rate cuts off the table for now. So the box the Fed is in has not changed: it can't comfortably cut into sticky inflation, and it doesn't want to hike into a softening labor market. What has changed is that, with less forward guidance from the chair, the incoming data does more of the talking. That's why one payroll print can move the whole conversation.

Third, and this is the headline healthcare story of the day: GLP-1 pricing has stopped being a clinical story and become a pricing and coverage story, and this month it reached the patient. As of July first, Medicare's fifty-dollar-a-month bridge copay for obesity drugs went live. Step back and look at the full price map: it runs from that fifty-dollar Medicare copay all the way up to a thirteen hundred and forty-nine-dollar Wegovy list price. Novo Nordisk has committed to a six hundred and seventy-five-dollar list across its three GLP-1s, roughly a fifty percent cut, and both Lilly and Novo are routing discounted product through the new government TrumpRx platform. Most importantly, Medicare is now covering obesity treatment for the first time, through a voluntary CMS demonstration. And waiting in the wings is a queue of oral GLP-1 challengers from AstraZeneca, Roche, Pfizer, Viking, and Structure. Remember the throughline we've been tracking: the molecule's price is collapsing while volume and coverage explode, which means the durable margin migrates downstream, to whoever owns oral manufacturing capacity and the distribution rails. The interesting question is no longer who owns the molecule. It's who owns the pipes.

That spread, between using AI and mastering it, is itself the investment thesis.

One more to round out the sweep, because it's the thread that sits underneath all the AI headlines: infrastructure capex. The running story this past month has been Samsung and SK Hynix committing on the order of a trillion-plus dollars to high-bandwidth memory, the HBM chips that feed AI accelerators. The tell there is where the bottleneck actually lives. It's not the model layer anymore, where capability is getting cheaper and more commoditized by the week. It's the physical layer, memory and compute. When you hear a trillion-dollar memory commitment, read it as the market pricing in years of demand for the picks and shovels, even as the software on top gets cheaper. That divergence, expensive infrastructure and deflating model prices, is one of the defining economic tensions of this AI cycle, and it's worth holding in your head whenever someone tells you AI is either all hype or all destiny. The truth is more specific: the money is moving to the layer that's hardest to replicate.

Now let's slow down for two deep dives.

Deep dive one: the Medicare obesity coverage story, and why the right historical lens is Medicare Part D in two thousand and six. When Medicare started covering outpatient prescription drugs for the first time under Part D in January two thousand six, it did three things. It reset demand curves, because a whole population that couldn't easily afford drugs suddenly could. It created durable, formulary-driven pricing power for whoever controlled distribution, which turned out to be the pharmacy benefit managers, the PBMs. And it expanded total volume even as per-unit economics compressed. Now map that onto today. Medicare covering obesity drugs for the first time in twenty twenty-six rhymes almost exactly. Expect the same pattern: an expansion in volume, and a migration of durable margin toward the distribution and PBM layer. Here's the strategic takeaway, and it's the kind of thing worth being able to say in a room full of investors. The headline everyone fixates on is the fifty-dollar copay. But the copay is not the structural shift. The structural shift is coverage expansion itself, because coverage is what resets the demand curve and rearranges who captures the economics. When a payer as large as Medicare decides to cover a category, the molecule gets commoditized by policy, and the interesting margin question moves to the rails, the manufacturing capacity and the distribution, not the drug. If you only remember one framework from today, make it this: first-time coverage is a volume-and-margin-migration event, not a price event.

Deep dive two: the adoption-versus-impact gap in enterprise AI, and the framework that explains it. McKinsey's argument, in plain terms, is that most organizations are measuring the wrong thing. They benchmark AI adoption, how many pilots they've deployed, how many seats they've licensed. Almost nobody benchmarks AI impact, meaning how many dollars actually moved on the P&L. The reason value doesn't show up is that companies layer AI onto broken workflows instead of redesigning the workflow around the agent. The framework here is what McKinsey calls rewiring, and it pairs neatly with the classic Three Horizons model. Under Three Horizons, Horizon one is your core business today, Horizon two is scaling emerging bets, and Horizon three is the speculative future. Agentic AI is currently graduating from Horizon two into Horizon one, from a scaling experiment toward core operations. But the governance debt, that sixty percent governance gap, is the tax that slows the crossing. Now connect it to healthcare specifically. The most quoted line this week was that decision quality does not equal outcomes. An AI tool can make a clinician's decision better on paper without changing what happens to the patient. And that gap matters enormously, because payers reimburse outcomes, not dashboards. Which is why the money in healthcare AI will likely flow first to the back office, revenue cycle management, prior authorization, utilization management, where an agent can touch a P&L line directly and provably, before it flows to clinical decision support, where the outcome link is murkier and the reimbursement case is harder to make. The investable thesis isn't the model. It's the operating-model surgery and the governance-and-orchestration layer that turns a demo into something a payer will fund.

There's a third strand worth a moment on this particular holiday, because the two-hundred-fiftieth-anniversary coverage is really a debate about American power, and that debate has direct market consequences. The Economist's framing, that America is mighty but becoming less dominant, is the analytical version of a question investors keep circling: is the dollar's role, and the pull of US capital markets, structurally eroding, or just cyclically wobbling? Compare it to the nineteen-nineties unipolar moment, when American economic and military primacy was essentially unchallenged. Today the picture is more contested, with a multipolar tilt in trade, technology, and supply chains. Why should you care beyond the op-ed page? Because the premium global investors pay to hold US assets, in bonds, in equities, in the dollar itself, rests partly on the assumption of American dominance. If that assumption softens gradually, it shows up not as a crash but as a slowly rising cost of capital and a slowly weakening currency, which feeds right back into the inflation-and-Fed story we opened with. Soft power and market power are more linked than they look, and a two-hundred-fiftieth birthday is a reasonable moment to take that seriously rather than sentimentally.

Let's connect today to the last few weeks, because these stories didn't start this morning.

Thread one, the Fed and the term premium. Go back to June seventeenth. That was Kevin Warsh's first meeting as chair, and the story then wasn't the fourth straight hold, it was that he inherited a fractured committee, the most dissents since the early nineteen-nineties, and a credibility test that we compared to Paul Volcker walking into the Fed in nineteen seventy-nine facing entrenched inflation. Warsh's guidance-light approach means that when a soft jobs number lands, the labor data itself, not the chair's words, moves the curve. That is exactly the regime a stripped-down Fed produces, and today's bad-news-is-good-news rally is that mechanism in action. This thread has now appeared nine times since early May. It's the single most durable macro narrative we're tracking.

Thread two, GLP-1 pricing and value migration. Back on June seventeenth, the story was Novo cutting list prices roughly seventy percent to about six hundred seventy-five dollars a month, effective January first, with Lilly's tirzepatide franchise pulling ahead at fifty-eight percent of US scripts versus Novo's forty-two, and tirzepatide projected above forty-five billion dollars in twenty twenty-six sales. Seven-plus appearances since May, and it has moved from abstract to concrete in real time.

Thread three, geopolitics and energy, which is the quiet anchor under the whole inflation story. On June twenty-ninth, the running thread was the Iran ceasefire that keeps almost breaking, both sides signaling they'd stand down after a fortnight of escalation, with ships halting in the Strait of Hormuz. Why does a healthcare-and-finance reader care? Because an open Hormuz keeps oil prices and inflation expectations anchored, and it's the energy-driven component of core inflation that's boxing the Fed in. Every time that ceasefire wobbles, the Fed's room to cut narrows. It's the connective tissue between the Middle East and your LBO financing spread.

And thread four, agentic AI adoption. On June seventeenth, the marker was JPMorgan reclassifying AI spend from experimental R&D to core infrastructure, roughly a twenty-billion-dollar technology budget for the year. That accounting change was the enterprise-adoption inflection point in bookkeeping form: AI became operating expense you can't cut, rather than a moonshot you can defer. Today's seventy-two-percent-in-production, sixty-percent-governance-gap data is the next node on that same line. The capability is no longer the bottleneck. Governance and orchestration are.

Thread five, the financing plumbing under private equity, because it's easy to forget the deals we keep discussing have to be funded by someone. In mid-June, McKinsey's read on private credit was that the asset class has matured from a deploy-capital land grab into a more competitive phase where spreads compress and the edge shifts from access-to-deals toward underwriting discipline and structuring. Here's the mechanism, since this is one worth internalizing: as more lenders chase the same deals, the yield premium they earn over public bonds, the illiquidity spread, shrinks, so returns increasingly depend on picking the right credit rather than simply being in the asset class at all. Why does that connect to today? Because private credit is now the financing backbone for the healthcare buyouts in that PwC and Bain data. When the Fed stays higher for longer, entry leverage costs more, and the sponsors who win are the ones underwriting cleanly rather than reaching for beta. The easy money in that trade is gone. What's left is skill.

If the FOMC holds again while payrolls keep softening, financing costs stay elevated but stable, and expectations for cuts later in twenty twenty-six revive. Two. Track the healthcare M&A rotation. PwC and Modern Healthcare both see health-services deal value and volume climbing through twenty twenty-six, with sponsors rotating away from reimbursement and regulatory exposure toward software and tech-enabled services, revenue cycle management, utilization management, AI telehealth. This extends the record twenty twenty-five for healthcare buyouts, which Bain pegged near one hundred ninety-one billion dollars in deal value across four hundred forty-five buyouts, with IT deal value doubling. The provider roll-up era is giving way to the tech-enabled-services era. If you're building or reviewing a healthcare investment thesis, that rotation is the backdrop.

Three. Watch the oral GLP-1 supply chain, not the injectable price war. With AstraZeneca, Roche, Pfizer, Viking, and Structure all queuing oral challengers, and Medicare obesity coverage scaling, the durable margin question is who owns oral manufacturing capacity and distribution. That's the Part D lesson applied forward.

Four. Watch the biotech IPO window. Scribe Therapeutics filing to go public, alongside Ipsen's Memo buyout, is an early tell on whether the capital markets are reopening for editing and oncology names. If the window cracks open, it changes the exit math for a lot of private biotech.

And a quieter one worth flagging, because it connects a June nineteenth thread to the platform-value question. That week, an FDA panel strongly backed Moderna's mRNA flu vaccine, and separately the FDA cleared the first continuous glucose monitor for children. Both are platform-expansion stories. The mRNA vote signals regulators are willing to extend the mRNA platform beyond COVID into the far larger recurring market of seasonal flu, which matters for how you value platform biotechs like Moderna and BioNTech, especially in a politically charged vaccine environment. And pediatric continuous glucose monitoring widens the addressable market for device makers like Dexcom and Abbott, a structural tailwind for the whole metabolic-health category that GLP-1s also sit in. The pattern to notice: in both drugs and devices, the durable value increasingly comes from extending a proven platform into an adjacent market, not from a single novel launch. That's the same logic as the serial-builder advantage McKinsey wrote about this week, applied to science.

And five. On the AI side, remember the maturity spread. Eighty-eight percent use it, roughly one percent have mastered it. Whether you're evaluating a company's AI story or building diligence questions, the sharp question is not "do you use AI," it's "can you point to a P&L line the AI moved, and what's your governance and orchestration layer." Adoption is table stakes now. Impact is the moat.

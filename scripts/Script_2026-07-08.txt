Good morning. A quick note before we start. The newsroom feed that normally hands me a fresh digest each morning has been quiet since the Fourth of July weekend, so today is a revisit and a synthesis. We are going to take the week's biggest threads, the ones that were still developing when markets closed for Independence Day, and carry them forward four days. That is actually a useful exercise. The best insights are rarely the ones that are twelve hours old. They are the ones you have watched mature over a week, where you can already see which way the story is bending. So think of this as the deep-read edition. Same rigor, a little more perspective.

Here is the single biggest story, and it is a framing story rather than a headline. We have quietly walked back into a bad-news-is-good-news market. In June, the US economy added just fifty-seven thousand jobs against a consensus estimate of around one hundred fifteen thousand. Roughly half of what Wall Street expected. And yet, on the session before the holiday, the S&P 500 closed essentially flat near seventy-four eighty-three, with the Dow at a record high. A badly missed jobs number, and stocks went up. If that strikes you as backwards, it is worth understanding exactly why, because it tells you what actually moves this market right now.

Let me explain the mechanism, because this is the kind of thing that sounds like jargon until someone unpacks it. When investors read economic data, they are not really reacting to the data. They are reacting to what the data will make the Federal Reserve do. That is called the Fed reaction function. Under Chair Warsh, this Fed has deliberately stripped back its forward guidance. It is not telling the market in advance what it plans to do. So the market has to infer the path from the incoming data itself. And with rates held at three and a half to three and three-quarters percent, the labor market has become the single swing variable. A weak jobs print lowers the odds of another hike at the July twenty-ninth meeting. Lower hike odds means cheaper money stays on the table longer. And cheaper money, or the promise of it, lifts asset prices. So a soft number is now read as dovish and therefore bullish, rather than as a warning about a slowing economy. Bad news is good news.

Now here is the wrinkle that makes this a genuinely good learning case. Unemployment actually fell in June, to four point two percent from four point three, even as hiring slowed to a crawl. How can both of those be true at once? The answer is the labor force. The unemployment rate is a fraction, and the denominator is the number of people actively looking for work. If people give up and stop looking, they drop out of that denominator, and the rate can fall even when almost no new jobs are being created. So a soft fifty-seven thousand and a friendly four point two percent can sit side by side. The lesson for reading these reports going forward: watch labor force participation, not just the headline rate. The headline can flatter you.

Odds going into late July sat roughly sixty-forty, hold versus hike, with energy-driven core inflation still sitting above target and keeping actual cuts off the table entirely. That is the tension to hold in your head. This is not an easing Fed. It is a paused Fed with an inflation problem, waiting to see whether the labor market cracks enough to justify patience.

Now let me slow down on the three stories that reward a deeper look, because each one comes with a framework or a piece of history that makes you sound like you have thought about it for longer than a news cycle.

The first is GLP-1 pricing, and this is the one to really understand, because it has now appeared in this feed something like seven times since May, and this week it crossed a threshold. As of July first, Medicare's fifty-dollar-a-month bridge copay for obesity drugs went live. That is not a proposal or a pilot on paper. That is a real copay that real patients are paying this month. At the same time, the pricing map spans an enormous range, from that fifty-dollar Medicare copay up to a Wegovy list price still north of thirteen hundred dollars, with Novo Nordisk committing to a six-hundred-seventy-five-dollar list across its three GLP-1s, roughly a fifty percent cut, and both Lilly and Novo routing discounted product through the new government TrumpRx platform. And crucially, Medicare is now covering obesity treatment for the first time, through a CMS voluntary demonstration.

Here is the framework, and it is historical. The single most useful parallel is Medicare Part D in 2006. That was the first time Medicare covered outpatient prescription drugs at all. And the Part D playbook is remarkably clean. When a giant public payer covers a category for the first time, three things happen. Volume expands dramatically, because a huge population that could not afford the product suddenly can. Per-unit economics compress, because the payer negotiates and list prices fall. And, most importantly, durable margin migrates away from the molecule and toward whoever controls distribution. In the Part D era, that was the pharmacy benefit managers. The drug got commoditized. The middle layer got rich.

So apply that lens to GLP-1s today. The obvious story is the price war, Novo cutting to defend share while Lilly's tirzepatide franchise pulls ahead in US prescriptions. But the more interesting question, the partner-level question, is not who owns the best molecule. It is who owns the oral manufacturing capacity and the distribution rails as this category commoditizes. There is a whole queue of oral GLP-1 challengers coming, from AstraZeneca, Roche, Pfizer, Viking, and Structure. The molecule is being turned into a policy and pricing story right in front of us. The durable margin is moving downstream. That is the shift that matters, and the copay headline is almost a distraction from it.

The second deep dive is the state of enterprise AI, and here the useful number is not adoption, it is the gap. McKinsey and the major analysts peg enterprise agentic AI at roughly seventy-two percent in production, but with something like a sixty percent governance gap. Sixty-five percent of enterprises are committing five to fifty million dollars a year to agents. And yet seventy to eighty percent of agentic initiatives still have not reached genuine enterprise scale. Sit with that. Nearly everyone is spending. Almost no one has crossed from pilot to scaled production.

The framework here is Three Horizons, McKinsey's old model for sequencing innovation. Horizon one is your core business, horizon two is scaling bets, horizon three is speculative options. Agentic AI is right now trying to graduate from horizon two, the scaling-bets zone, down into horizon one, the core-business zone. And the thing slowing that crossing is not the models. The models are fine. It is governance debt. The orchestration, the observability, the guardrails, the human-in-the-loop controls that turn an impressive demo into something you can put a service-level agreement around. And that is the actual investment thesis hiding in the numbers. If eighty-eight percent of firms use AI but only a sliver are mature, then the money is not in building a better model. It is in selling the governance and orchestration layer that lets everyone else close the pilot-to-production gap. The maturity gap is the opportunity.

There is a related McKinsey point on healthcare specifically that sharpens this. McKinsey's line is that healthcare's productivity problem will not be solved by more hiring or more technology. It needs redesigned human-AI workflows. Their survey has eighty-five percent of healthcare leaders exploring or adopting generative AI, and sixty-four percent reporting realized or anticipated positive return. But the value comes from rewiring the workflow around the agent, not bolting the agent onto an unchanged process. And notice where the uptake is strongest: knowledge management, IT, revenue cycle, prior authorization, utilization management. The back office. Which makes sense, because payers fund outcomes, not dashboards, and the back-office automation touches a profit-and-loss line before clinical AI ever will. The talking point I would keep in my pocket: everyone benchmarks AI adoption, almost no one benchmarks AI impact, and the alpha is in the operating-model redesign that lets the model actually touch the P&L.

The third deep dive ties the first two together, and it is about where healthcare private equity is pointing. PwC and Modern Healthcare both see health-services deal value and volume climbing through 2026. But look at the composition. Sponsors are rotating away from reimbursement and regulatory exposure, away from the physician-practice roll-ups that get squeezed by drug pricing and rate cuts, and toward software and tech-enabled services. Revenue-cycle management, utilization management, AI telehealth. This extends a thread from the record 2025, which saw a hundred ninety-one billion dollars of healthcare buyouts across four hundred forty-five deals, with healthcare IT deal value roughly doubling. The rotation from provider roll-ups to tech-enabled services is the through-line. And you can see why it rhymes with the GLP-1 story and the AI story: in an uncertain rate and policy environment, capital wants assets with less direct exposure to the things it cannot control, and more exposure to the automation layer that gets more valuable as everyone tries to cut cost.

There is a useful lens for organizing all of this, which is to think about where the durable moat sits in any healthcare asset. Broadly there are four. A scientific moat, meaning proprietary drug or device intellectual property. A regulatory moat, meaning FDA position, payer contracts, or scope-of-practice protection. A delivery moat, meaning ownership of the patient relationship, the site of care, or the outcomes data. And a financing moat, meaning a genuine cost-of-capital advantage at scale. What you are watching this cycle is capital rotating away from assets whose only moat was regulatory or reimbursement-based, because those moats are exactly what drug pricing resets and rate volatility erode, and toward assets that combine a delivery moat with a financing moat, which is what tech-enabled services with recurring revenue and light regulatory exposure actually offer. If you can name which of the four moats an asset relies on, you can usually predict how it behaves when the policy weather turns. And right now the weather is turning against the reimbursement-dependent and toward the automation-and-data-dependent.

Let me pull the cross-temporal threads together, because this is where a week of watching pays off.

Thread one, the Fed transition. This has now shown up nine times since May. The consistent story is that a guidance-light Warsh Fed has handed the steering wheel to the data and to the term premium, rather than to its own forward guidance. The term premium, quickly, is the extra yield investors demand to hold longer-dated bonds instead of rolling short ones, essentially their price for uncertainty. When the Fed stops telling you what it will do, that uncertainty premium does more of the work in setting long rates. So June's soft payrolls did not just move sentiment. In this regime, the labor data itself is the mechanism moving the curve. That is why the reaction function matters more than any single print.

Thread two, energy and geopolitics, which connects straight back to what we read at the end of June. Around June twenty-ninth, the story was America and Iran signaling they would stand down after two weeks of escalation, strikes on US sites, ships hesitating in the Strait of Hormuz. The reason that thread matters for everything else is simple: an open Hormuz keeps oil flowing and keeps inflation expectations anchored, which is precisely what lets the Fed even consider being patient. The thing to watch is whether stand down actually holds, or whether it is the third false dawn in a month. Energy-driven core inflation is the one input that could take cuts off the table entirely, so this geopolitical thread and the Fed thread are really the same thread wearing two hats.

Thread three, the AI capex question. The developing narrative has quietly shifted. Two months ago the framing was AI capital spending is bullish. Now the framing is, what if the capex does not earn its return. Samsung and SK Hynix alone have committed something on the order of one point three trillion dollars to high-bandwidth memory. The bottleneck for AI is now memory and compute, not the model layer. And back in late June we saw the first market wobbles on exactly this AI-return anxiety. This is the classic overinvestment question that has preceded every prior technology drawdown, from railways to fiber optic to the dot-com build-out. It does not mean a crash is coming. It means the burden of proof is shifting from the enthusiasts to the spenders, and that shift is usually where volatility lives.

There is also the open-versus-closed model thread worth flagging alongside the capex question. Late June brought the release of a strong open-weights model that some called a DeepSeek moment for agentic coding, and the cost curve keeps bending toward open weights. This matters for the return-on-capex debate: if open models keep closing the gap with closed frontier ones, then the trillion dollars going into proprietary infrastructure has to earn its return against a free alternative that is only a few months behind. That is precisely the kind of dynamic that makes investors nervous about whether the spending pencils out.

And there is a quieter fifth thread worth naming, the decision-quality-versus-outcomes gap. We saw it in late June with a study showing a generative AI tool improved clinical decision-making but not patient outcomes, and it echoes in this week's McKinsey healthcare piece. This is the crux of healthcare value and access strategy. Payers reimburse outcomes, not better decisions and not prettier dashboards. Any healthcare AI thesis that cannot draw a line from the tool to a measured outcome is going to struggle to monetize. Hold onto that, because it is the question that quietly decides which healthcare AI companies actually get paid.

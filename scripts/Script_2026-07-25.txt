Good morning, Anurag. This is your Daily Reading for Saturday, July twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-six. It's the weekend deep-read edition, so today runs a little more reflective: fewer headlines, more connective tissue.

Here is the single biggest story. All week the market has been asking one question: what is the return on a dollar of AI spending? Yesterday that question showed up as Alphabet's first-ever negative free cash flow and a roughly eight hundred and ninety billion dollar megacap wipeout. Today, McKinsey answers it with data. In a new study of four hundred and seventy-one private-equity-backed companies across thirty-one industries, McKinsey finds that companies that embed AI into their products and build new AI businesses trade at a median revenue multiple roughly one hundred and thirty percent higher than companies using AI only for internal productivity. The market, in other words, pays for AI that changes what you sell, and pays almost nothing for AI that changes how you operate. That single finding is the Rosetta Stone for this entire week of price action, and we'll spend real time on it in the deep dives.

First, the news sweep.

Item one, the McKinsey private-equity study itself, because the structure matters as much as the headline. McKinsey lays out what it calls a value creation ladder with four levels. Level one is opportunistic pilots, scattered copilots and chatbots. Level two is operating-model enhancement, AI wired into core workflows to take out cost. Level three is product transformation, AI changing the actual thing the customer buys. Level four is AI business-building, standing up new AI-native revenue lines. Here's the striking part: companies at levels one and two trade around thirteen to fourteen times revenue, roughly the same as companies doing nothing at all. The multiple only jumps at levels three and four, reaching a median of thirty-one times, and thirty-three times for software companies. The market is drawing a bright line between AI as a cost tool and AI as a product, and it is paying zero premium for the former. One more practical detail worth stealing: McKinsey flags the rent-versus-buy AI-talent decision as a core value-creation lever. Whether a portfolio company hires machine-learning engineers or rents capability through vendors is now a deal-team decision, not an IT decision.

Item two, the Wall Street Journal reports that corporate America has, in its words, suddenly decided to stop blowing money on AI. Companies large and small are mixing cheaper models with premium frontier models rather than defaulting to the most expensive option for every task. Think of it the way a manufacturer thinks about materials: you don't build every component out of titanium. The reset landed the same week as that eight hundred and ninety billion dollar tech wipeout, and the Journal's framing is blunt: Wall Street is confronting the fact that the biggest tech companies are no longer cash-printing machines. Put items one and two together and the story tells itself. Buyers are optimizing spend at exactly the moment the market stops rewarding sellers for capex. The efficiency turn is the level-one-and-two trap in action: cost discipline is table stakes now, not value creation.

Item three, McKinsey's companion piece on B2B sales makes the same argument from the go-to-market side. AI sales pilots are everywhere, but value is not. The winners, McKinsey argues, rewire entire commercial impact journeys with agentic AI, redesigning how a customer is identified, engaged, converted, and expanded, so that sellers spend their recovered time deepening relationships rather than doing administration. This extends the B2B Pulse finding from yesterday, that high-growth firms are three times more likely to have raised AI investment by double digits. Same lesson, sharper edge: bolting an agent onto the old funnel is a pilot; rewiring the journey is a strategy.

Item four, healthcare. McKinsey senior partner Patrick Finn argues on the firm's podcast that healthcare is at a uniquely unstable moment, and that the tension in the system, the friction between payers, providers, and pharma, is precisely what could force its reinvention. His sharpest claim is that AI may reshape who leads the sector's next era, not just how it runs. For your purposes, "who leads next" is an investor question. Whether incumbents or AI-native entrants end up on top is where healthcare buyout theses will be made or broken over the next three years. If the McKinsey PE study says value accrues to firms that change what they sell, Finn is asking which healthcare players are structurally capable of doing that. Most incumbents are trapped at level two by design: their margins live in administrative complexity, and genuinely transforming the product would cannibalize the complexity they monetize.

Item five, the macro picture, and it's a two-parter. The Economist reports that fewer investors than ever believe central bankers will return inflation to the two percent target, and markets are repricing for a structurally higher-inflation world. Meanwhile the Journal reports a government-bond selloff that has pushed the ten-year Treasury yield near its twenty twenty-six high, tightening financial conditions days before the Fed meets on July twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth. Here's the mechanism that links them, and it's foundational: when investors stop believing inflation will return to target, they demand extra yield on long bonds as compensation for holding money that may lose purchasing power. Economists call expectations becoming un-anchored. Un-anchored expectations feed directly into long-term yields regardless of what the Fed does with the short rate, and the long-term yield is the discount rate for every risk asset on the planet. We'll come back to this in deep dive two, because it's the second blade of a two-bladed squeeze on asset prices.

Item six, Blackstone's profit surged on AI investments, with stronger inflows into its private-equity business offsetting a slowdown in private credit, where retail investors have pulled back. Yesterday we covered the numbers: distributable earnings up twenty-six percent, record assets under management around one point three-five trillion dollars, and nine of Blackstone's ten highest-appreciating positions AI-related. Today's addition is the composition question: retail money retreating from private credit specifically. Hold that thought for the threads section, because it completes a story that started in March.

Item seven, The Economist asks whether China can dominate AI exports the way it came to dominate electric vehicles and solar panels, with robotaxis as the early test case. The playbook is familiar: subsidize domestic scale, brutalize domestic margins through competition, then export the survivors at prices nobody else can match. The open question is whether AI works the same way, because AI exports carry something EVs don't: data governance, national security review, and trust. A Chinese robotaxi in Jakarta is a hardware sale; a Chinese foundation model inside a Gulf sovereign-wealth fund's infrastructure is a geopolitical event. Watch this because it decides whether the AI market bifurcates into two spheres the way telecom equipment did after Huawei.

Item eight, a healthcare governance story that's easy to skim past and shouldn't be. The Journal reports that Taylor Farms, the produce supplier, called the White House to try to delay an FDA cyclospora recall before the agency went public. Regardless of how it resolves, it's a live case study in regulatory capture risk, industry pressure reaching into the referee's booth. If you're underwriting anything in food, pharma, or medical supply, the assumption that the regulator functions independently is itself a diligence item now. It ties directly to the governance themes in your LGST coursework, and it rhymes with yesterday's FDA advisory panel overriding its own career scientists on peptide compounding. Now the deep dives. Three today.

Deep dive one: the McKinsey value creation ladder, and the century-old economics underneath it. The finding that markets pay nothing for productivity AI sounds strange until you meet the productivity paradox. In nineteen ninety, the economic historian Paul David studied the electrification of American factories and found something remarkable: it took roughly forty years after the electric motor arrived for productivity gains to show up in the data. Why? Because the first generation of factory owners simply swapped their steam engines for electric motors and changed nothing else. The factory was still built vertically around a central power shaft. The gains only came when a new generation redesigned the factory horizontally, single-story plants, machines arranged by workflow rather than by proximity to the drive shaft, which unlocked the assembly line. The technology was necessary; the reorganization was the value. That is precisely the pattern McKinsey's data is showing at level three and four of the ladder. Buying copilots is swapping the steam engine for a motor. Rewiring the product and the business around AI is rebuilding the factory floor. And the market, unlike in nineteen ninety, has a real-time scoreboard: it's called the revenue multiple, and it's telling you the reorganizers are worth thirty-one times and the adopters are worth fourteen. One caution before you quote the study at a dinner: this is correlation, not causation. Companies capable of climbing to level four likely have better management, better data assets, and better products to begin with, so some of that multiple premium is a marker of quality, not a prize you win just by shipping an AI feature. McKinsey itself gates level three on two prerequisites, a proprietary data moat and an integrated product, data, and engineering operating model. Absent those, pushing a portfolio company up the ladder destroys value rather than creating it. The honest version of the talking point is: the ladder tells you where value shows up, not that any company can climb it.

Deep dive two: the de-anchoring of inflation expectations, and why it matters more than the Fed's actual decision next week. Since the nineteen-nineties, the entire architecture of central banking has rested on one achievement: convincing markets that inflation would always return to roughly two percent. That belief was self-fulfilling. If everyone expects two percent, workers moderate wage demands, companies moderate price increases, and lenders accept modest yields, so inflation obligingly returns to two. The Economist's report says that belief is eroding. This is the lesson of the nineteen-seventies in reverse: back then, a decade of supply shocks, the OPEC embargoes we discussed yesterday, taught workers and firms to expect high inflation, and expectations became embedded in wage-price spirals that took Volcker's twenty percent interest rates to break. The current setup has an uncomfortable amount of rhyme: three simultaneous supply-side pressures, oil above one hundred dollars, tariffs on more than twenty countries, and the memory-chip crunch, hitting an economy where the fiscal deficit keeps supplying demand. The ten-year yield pushing toward its twenty twenty-six high is the bond market pricing exactly this: not next week's Fed decision, but the durability of the two percent promise itself. And here's why it lands on your desk: the long yield is the risk-free anchor in every discounted-cash-flow model and every leveraged-buyout financing assumption. Yesterday we walked through how a move from four percent to four point seven percent punishes long-duration assets. Today's twist is nastier: if the move reflects de-anchored expectations rather than a temporary oil spike, it doesn't mean-revert when the tanker situation calms down. A structurally higher discount rate is a permanent haircut to private-market marks that were underwritten in a four-percent world. The Fed can look through an oil shock. It cannot look through the market ceasing to believe in the target, because that belief is the only tool that works without breaking something.

Deep dive three, shorter: Blackstone as a tell for where the AI cycle is. Assemble the pieces from the last three weeks. Blackstone and Brookfield sell physical data-center assets into the boom. JPMorgan projects the five biggest data-center builders hit zero combined free cash flow by the third quarter. Alphabet prints negative free cash flow. Read as one narrative: the most sophisticated capital allocator in private markets has rotated out of AI's concrete, out of the lending exposure, and into the equity of the application and model layer, and its earnings are now being paid by that rotation. The retail pullback from private credit is the crowd belatedly following the smart money out of the same door. If you want a one-line summary of the AI trade in July twenty twenty-six, it's this: the builders are being repriced, the lenders are being drained, and the owners of what runs on top are being rewarded. Same telecom-overbuild logic we walked through yesterday, executed by the people with the best information in the market.

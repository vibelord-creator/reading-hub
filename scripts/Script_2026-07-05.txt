Here is the single most important idea, and it is worth restating slowly because it reframes everything else. We have quietly walked back into a bad-news-is-good-news market. In June, the American economy added just fifty-seven thousand jobs, roughly half of what economists had penciled in at around one hundred and fifteen thousand. And stocks went up. The S&P 500 closed essentially flat at seven thousand four hundred and eighty-three on July second, with the Dow at a record, heading into the holiday. In a normal world, a jobs report that misses by half spooks equity investors who worry the economy is stalling. Instead it cheered them, because a soft labor market lowers the odds that the Federal Reserve raises rates again at its meeting on July twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth. When a market starts treating bad economic news as good market news, that is not noise. It is telling you that the Fed's reaction function has become the entire game. The data matters only to the extent that it moves the central bank. Hold that thought, because nearly everything today loops back to it.

Let us do the news sweep, and I will keep each item tight.

First, the jobs number itself, because it is subtler than the headline. The economy added fifty-seven thousand jobs against a consensus near one hundred and fifteen thousand, and yet the unemployment rate fell, to four point two percent from four point three. How can hiring slow and unemployment drop in the same month? Because the unemployment rate is a ratio, and its denominator, the labor force, can shrink. If enough people stop actively looking for work, they are no longer counted as unemployed, and the rate can tick down even as job creation weakens. So a genuinely soft report and a lower jobless rate can live side by side. The lesson to carry into any conversation is this: watch labor force participation, not just the headline rate. This single variable is the swing factor for the whole path of interest rates right now, and therefore for the cost of financing a leveraged buyout, and for how anyone underwrites a healthcare deal this year.

Second, the Fed backdrop. The Federal Open Market Committee has held its target range at three and a half to three and three-quarters percent for four straight meetings, under the new chair Kevin Warsh, who has run a deliberately guidance-light operation. Odds into late July sit at roughly sixty-forty in favor of another hold rather than a hike, with energy-driven core inflation still sitting above target and keeping outright rate cuts off the table for now. The mechanism to understand here is why a Fed might stay on hold, or even flirt with hiking, when growth is clearly cooling. The answer is stagflation risk: when inflation is sticky and driven by supply, mainly energy, cutting rates to support a weak economy risks pouring fuel on prices. So the Fed waits, and the labor data becomes the thing everyone watches to see which way it breaks.

Third, on the geopolitical and soft-power front, the weekend coverage was framed by America's two hundred and fiftieth birthday, its semiquincentennial. The Economist ran an anniversary essay on the transatlantic relationship, paired with a harder-edged piece arguing that America is mighty but becoming less dominant. That is worth reading through a specific lens: the difference between military and economic might on one hand, and dominance on the other. A country can grow its absolute power while its relative share of the global system erodes, simply because others are catching up. That is the hegemonic-decline debate, and it has a clean historical anchor we will come back to.

Fourth, in healthcare and life sciences, Roche's divarasib beat its KRAS G12C rivals in late-stage non-small-cell lung cancer, resetting the bar in a closely watched competitive set. Worth a word on why that matters beyond the trial result: KRAS was for decades considered one of the great undruggable targets in oncology, so a molecule that not only hits it but bests the incumbents is both a scientific milestone and a competitive reshuffle. It is a read-through for the entire KRAS competitive set and for how quickly a first-mover advantage in a hard target can be eroded. Separately, a pan-cancer artificial intelligence tool showed it can improve immunotherapy response prediction, and the United Kingdom moved to expand access to Wegovy. Two of the sector's biggest structural stories, patient selection by algorithm and access expansion for GLP-1s, in a single day's wrap. And keep an eye on Eric Topol's writing this week on a blood test that reads the tumor microenvironment, which sits adjacent to the liquid-biopsy race and to the broader question of whether we can catch and characterize cancer earlier and less invasively.

Sixth, on artificial intelligence, the frontier cadence continued. OpenAI rolled out its GPT-5.6 family with new methods for training robots and, more interestingly, models that invoke other models, the agentic pattern of software calling software. Anthropic shipped Sonnet 5, a cheaper flagship tier, and described research tooling that runs against more than sixty databases at once. The throughline is that the model layer keeps getting cheaper and more capable, which pushes the value, and the scarcity, somewhere else. Seventh, on deal flow, both PwC and Modern Healthcare see health-services merger-and-acquisition value and volume climbing through twenty twenty-six, as asset quality improves and sponsors rotate away from reimbursement and regulatory exposure toward software and tech-enabled services: revenue-cycle management, utilization management, and AI-enabled telehealth. That rotation matters. It says the smart money in healthcare private equity is moving from owning the provider, with all its rate-cut and labor-cost exposure, toward owning the software layer that sits on top of many providers. This extends the record set in twenty twenty-five, when healthcare private equity did around one hundred and ninety-one billion dollars of disclosed deal value across four hundred and forty-five buyouts, with information-technology deal value roughly doubling. The rotation from provider roll-ups to tech-enabled services is not a blip, it is the direction of travel.

And eighth, a quieter McKinsey note worth filing away: the serial builder advantage. Repeat founders and repeat innovators compound their edge by building, learning, and building again. For anyone in private equity or venture, this is a reminder that the pattern-recognition you should prize in a management team is not a single win, it is evidence of a repeatable process. A team on its third company knows things a first-timer cannot, and that knowledge is an underpriced asset.

That is the sweep. Now let us go deeper on the two threads that actually matter, and give each one a framework and a piece of history so it sticks.

The first deep dive is GLP-1 pricing, and this is the story I would master if I were you, because it sits exactly on the value, access, and pricing terrain of the healthcare strategy world. As of July first, Medicare began covering obesity treatment for the first time, through a voluntary demonstration from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. The price and access map now stretches from a fifty-dollar-a-month Medicare bridge copay at one end to a thirteen hundred and forty-nine-dollar Wegovy list price at the other. Novo Nordisk has committed to a six hundred and seventy-five-dollar list price across its three GLP-1 products, roughly a fifty percent cut, and both Novo and Eli Lilly are routing discounted product through a new government platform branded TrumpRx. Meanwhile a wave of oral GLP-1 challengers, from AstraZeneca, Roche, Pfizer, Viking, and Structure, is queuing up behind the injectables.

Here is the framework to impose on all of that. The GLP-1 story has stopped being a clinical story and become a pricing-and-coverage story. When a molecule works and everyone can see it works, the competition stops being about efficacy and starts being about who controls cost and distribution. The molecule gets commoditized by policy, and the durable margin migrates downstream, to whoever owns oral manufacturing at scale and the distribution rails that get product to patients. So the interesting investment question is no longer who owns the best peptide. It is who owns the pipes.

And now the history, which is what turns a talking point into a partner talking point. Medicare covering obesity drugs for the first time in twenty twenty-six rhymes precisely with the launch of Medicare Part D in two thousand six, which was the first time Medicare covered outpatient prescription drugs at all. Part D reset the entire demand curve. It expanded volume enormously, and it created durable, formulary-driven pricing power for whoever sat at the distribution chokepoint, which turned out to be the pharmacy benefit managers. The per-unit economics compressed, but the total pool of value grew, and it accrued to the middlemen who controlled access. Apply that lens to GLP-1s and the prediction almost writes itself: volume expansion plus margin migration to the distribution and PBM layer. The headline copay is the noise. The coverage expansion is the signal. And there is a second-order move layered on top this time that Part D did not have, the arrival of a government purchasing and distribution platform, TrumpRx, routing discounted product directly. That is a new node in the value chain, and new nodes are exactly where power and margin get quietly redistributed. If you want a single question to carry into a healthcare conversation, make it this: in a world where Medicare now buys these drugs and a federal platform helps distribute them, who actually captures the economics five years out, the manufacturer, the PBM, or the platform? Nobody knows yet, and that uncertainty is precisely why it is the interesting question.

The second deep dive is agentic artificial intelligence, and the key is to separate adoption from impact, because that gap is the whole thesis. McKinsey's read this week is that enterprise agentic AI is now roughly seventy-two percent in production, but with something like a sixty percent governance gap, and that seventy to eighty percent of agentic initiatives still have not reached genuine enterprise scale. Sit with that contradiction. Eighty-eight percent of firms use AI in some form. Something like one percent describe themselves as mature. Gartner and Forrester both frame twenty twenty-six as the breakthrough year for multi-agent systems, meaning specialized agents coordinated under a central orchestrator, and McKinsey reports that sixty-five percent of enterprises are now committing five to fifty million dollars a year to agents.

The framework here is the Three Horizons model, one of the classic McKinsey structures. Horizon one is your core business today. Horizon two is the emerging, scaling bets. Horizon three is the genuinely new. Agentic AI is graduating from Horizon two, where you scale a promising bet, toward Horizon one, where it becomes core infrastructure. But the governance debt, the missing observability, policy engines, and human-in-the-loop controls, is the tax that slows that crossing. And this connects straight to healthcare, because McKinsey's companion argument this week was that the sector's productivity problem will not be solved by more hiring or more technology. It will be solved by redesigning the workflow around the agent, what they call human-AI workflows, rather than bolting a tool onto an existing process. Its survey found eighty-five percent of healthcare leaders exploring or adopting generative AI and sixty-four percent reporting realized or anticipated positive return, with the strongest uptake in knowledge management and back-office functions.

Put the partner point plainly. Everyone benchmarks AI adoption. Almost nobody benchmarks AI impact. The alpha is not the model, because the model is getting cheaper by the month. The alpha is the operating-model redesign that lets the model actually touch a line on the profit-and-loss statement. In healthcare, that is why the money shows up first in revenue-cycle management, prior authorization, and utilization management, the agentic back office, and only later in clinical AI. Payers fund outcomes, not better dashboards. The gap between a working demo and a service-level agreement is exactly where the enablement spending, on governance and orchestration, gets captured.

The model is not the moat.

One more piece of strategic context, since the weekend framing invited it. The Economist's argument that America is mighty but becoming less dominant has a clean historical anchor: the unipolar moment of the nineteen nineties. After the Soviet collapse, the United States enjoyed a decade of near-unchallenged primacy, and the mistake of that era was to treat a moment as a permanent condition. Go back further and you find the same pattern in the British Empire around nineteen hundred, still the largest economy and navy in the world, yet already being out-grown in relative terms by the United States and Germany. The lesson that travels across both cases is that absolute power and relative dominance are different things, and they can move in opposite directions at the same time. A hegemon can keep getting stronger while its share of the world shrinks, because others are compounding faster. For an investor, the practical translation is to watch relative growth rates and share shifts, not just absolute size, when you assess any incumbent, whether it is a country, a company, or a category leader like a frontier AI lab.

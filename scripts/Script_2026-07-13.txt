First, the news sweep.

A reimposed blockade plus a twenty percent cargo fee is a new hybrid instrument: part military action, part tariff. And here's the mechanism worth internalizing: this is a supply shock, not demand inflation. Raising interest rates cannot create oil. So if the Fed tightens into this — and the June minutes suggest a hawkish bias — you get slower growth and higher prices at the same time. That combination is stagflation, and it's why an oil spike can be bearish for stocks and bonds simultaneously. For your private equity lens, it's a dual squeeze: discount rates go up while healthcare-provider margins get compressed by energy and supply costs.

Analysts read the suit as sand in the gears rather than a knockout blow — the same litigation playbook Apple ran against Samsung starting in 2010. The signal for investors: the AI value war is moving from models to devices and distribution, and litigation is now a competitive weapon you have to price into any AI-adjacent thesis.

Item four, the AI memory crunch has reached consumers. The read-through: AI capital spending is quietly becoming a broad inflationary input, not just a chip-stock story. Item five, a caution on the earnings math behind that data-center story. It's worth being honest about the counterargument before accepting the bearish framing: free cash flow going to zero during a buildout is not by itself a red flag — it's what aggressive investment looks like on a cash-flow statement. Amazon ran negative free cash flow for years while building the infrastructure that became AWS. The difference that matters is contract quality: if the compute is pre-leased to investment-grade counterparties on long tenors, the builders are effectively creating bond-like assets and the funding gap is a financing problem, not a solvency problem. If the demand is merchant — spot-priced, cancellable, concentrated in a few AI labs burning venture money — then the 2000s telecom comparison earns its keep. The honest answer is that today's reporting doesn't settle which one this is. Hold both hypotheses.

Now the deep dives. Three today.

Deep dive one: the Hormuz shock against 1973 and 1979.

The historical ledger here matters because central banks have gotten this exact situation wrong before. The 1973 OPEC embargo removed roughly seven percent of world supply and produced the stagflation that defined the decade. The 1979 shock brought Volcker's brutal rate response — necessary by then, but a reminder that policy arrives late and hits hard. Your Key Themes database has flagged before that a full Hormuz closure would be roughly four times the 1973 embargo in supply terms, which is why even partial disruption moves prices this violently.

Today's version has two genuinely new features. First, the United States is now a net energy exporter, so the domestic hit is cushioned in a way it never was in the seventies — the pain concentrates in Europe, Japan, India, and China, which changes the geopolitical bargaining table. Second, the twenty percent cargo fee is something the seventies never saw: a tariff layered on top of a blockade, turning a security instrument into a revenue-and-leverage instrument. Nobody knows how markets price a fee-based chokepoint — is it a one-time level shift in shipping costs, or a persistent tax on global trade?

The lesson worth respecting from 1979: central banks that misread supply shocks as demand inflation over-tighten and deepen the downturn. Watch whether Fed communication this week distinguishes energy pass-through from core momentum. If it doesn't, the policy-error scenario gets more probable. And to be fair to the other side: the hawks' argument is that inflation expectations are already unanchored enough that even a supply shock has to be met with credibility-defending tightness. That's the actual debate — not hawks versus doves, but which error is cheaper.

Deep dive two: McKinsey's operating-model argument, and why three separate reads this week are the same thesis.

McKinsey's July 7th piece, Winning the race to rewire in 2026, argues the durable AI advantage isn't the model — it's redesigning how work gets done and how decisions get made, end to end. Winners rewire the operating model: people, process, and decision rights, with technology as the enabler rather than the headline. This is the Rewired framework — six capabilities culminating in operating-model redesign — and it pairs with the benchmark from the AI Transformation Manifesto in your Key Themes: three dollars of EBITDA impact for every dollar of AI spend as the bar for a program that's actually working.

Now set three documents side by side. Gartner says 234 billion dollars of SaaS spend is at risk because agents collapse seat-based workflows — that's the threat sizing. McKinsey's rewire piece says the winners are the ones who redesign decision-making around the new tools — that's the winner's playbook. And the McKinsey Global Banking Review from your backlog quantifies the stakes in one industry: banks that fail to rewire risk about 170 billion dollars, roughly nine percent of the global profit pool, over the decade, while AI pioneers could add up to four percentage points of return on tangible equity. Same value migration, three vantage points: the model layer commoditizes; the operating layer captures the value.

For your PE-operating-partner lens, the healthcare application is the McKinsey generative-AI-in-healthcare ROI piece still sitting in your catch-up list: nine to fifteen percent of national health expenditure as the prize, providers gaining eleven to seventeen percent of gross revenue through prior-auth and coding automation, payers cutting admin costs up to twenty-five percent. Those numbers are the underwriting math behind tech-enabled healthcare buyouts — but the rewire piece is the reason most of those deals will miss: value shows up only when a sponsor redesigns the post-close P&L and decision rights, not when it buys another point solution. If you take one partner talking point from today, make it this: everyone benchmarks AI spend; the winners benchmark decision latency.

Deep dive three: the AI infrastructure funding math.

Walk the chain. Hyperscaler capex approaches a trillion dollars this year. JPMorgan sees a 1.4 trillion dollar funding gap between committed buildouts and internally generated cash. The five largest builders' combined free cash flow trends to zero by Q3. Meanwhile the memory bottleneck — Samsung and SK Hynix diverting capacity to HBM — is pushing DRAM prices up thirty percent and forcing Apple to raise consumer prices. And the most sophisticated owners of physical AI assets, Blackstone and Brookfield, are selling stabilized assets into the demand.

Here's the finance mechanism underneath: when a sector's growth outruns its cash generation, the funding has to come from somewhere — debt, asset sales, or equity issuance. Each has a cost, and all three costs rise with rates. That's why the oil shock and the hawkish Fed matter directly for the AI trade: if rates stay higher for longer, the 1.4 trillion gap gets more expensive to bridge exactly as free cash flow hits zero. The dot-com parallel isn't the demand story — demand for AI compute is real, just as demand for bandwidth was real in 2000. The parallel is the financing structure: telecom carriers built ahead of monetization with borrowed money, and when capital markets repriced, the builders went bankrupt while the users of cheap bandwidth — the Googles and Amazons — captured the value. Watch who is quietly moving from builder to lender.

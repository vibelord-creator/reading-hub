A quick macro basics refresher because this comes up in every conversation. Stagflation means stagnant growth combined with persistent inflation. Rate cuts normally stimulate growth, but in a stagflation regime they risk re-anchoring inflation expectations higher. The central bank loses its anti-inflation credibility, and the long end of the yield curve resteepens. That is the most painful path for duration-heavy portfolios, including most leveraged buyouts. The 1979 analog is Paul Volcker replacing William Miller under stagflation conditions and choosing recession over inflation. Warsh's choice in 2026 is the closest analog in forty-seven years.

For private equity, the practical translation is sharp. Stagflation breaks the standard PE playbook. Multiple expansion vanishes because cost of capital stays high. Leverage costs do not come down. Operating leverage gets eaten by wage stickiness and energy pass-through. The most fragile cohort is the 2021 vintage, underwritten to two percent inflation and falling rates. Now the deep dives.

Deep dive one. The historical parallel between the current Fed transition and the 1979 Volcker pivot is tighter than people are saying out loud. Volcker replaced G. William Miller in August 1979 under a stagflation regime, with energy prices spiking, inflation un-anchored, and a Fed perceived as politically captured under the Burns and Miller chairs. Volcker's response was the credibility trade. He took rates to twenty percent and forced a recession. The breakeven cost of restoring Fed credibility was a deep recession. Powell-to-Warsh in May 2026 is the closest institutional analog the United States has seen since. Stagflation conditions are confirmed by Apollo, Bridgewater, and now the Wall Street Journal economist panel. Political pressure to cut is explicit. The energy shock from the Strait of Hormuz remains a tail input. The question is whether Warsh chooses the Miller path, which is to capitulate to political pressure and lose credibility, or the Volcker path, which is to hold or hike and accept the recession cost.

For private equity, the difference between those two paths is the difference between LBO refinancing at par or distressed. Most 2021-vintage healthcare LBOs were underwritten to a two-percent terminal cost of debt and falling rates. The Volcker path keeps cost of debt elevated, forces extensions and amend-and-extends, and produces the second wave of debt-to-equity conversions like the Blackstone-KKR Affordable Care flip we discussed in story two. The Miller path produces a near-term refinancing window but at the cost of a re-rated inflation regime that destroys terminal value. There is no easy path. Warsh's tested instinct, per his 2010 dissent against quantitative easing, is hawkish. The market is not fully priced for that.

Deep dive two. McKinsey's M&A integrations piece from last weekend, which we covered Sunday, codifies three plays for the current valuation environment. Roll-ups, with the insurance broker case at eighty-plus acquisitions and a ten-times exit multiple at twenty-five percent IRR. Combinations of comparably sized portfolio companies, with an ad-tech case showing twenty-seven percent G-and-A reduction. And complementary-capabilities combinations, the utilities case. The data point that should worry every sponsor is that unsuccessful large combinations see a three percent pro-forma revenue dip post-close. Three percent sounds small. At PE leverage levels, run through fixed costs and interest coverage, it is an entire year of equity returns gone. The implication is that integration execution is now the dominant alpha source in private equity. Multiple expansion is gone. Leverage is more expensive. Operational improvement is the only lever left, and the granularity of operational improvement runs through integration discipline.

Tying these together. McKinsey's PE 2026 report shows that the industry is maturing, with secondaries and semiliquids becoming the new growth vector. McKinsey's M&A integrations piece shows that returns now come from execution, not financial engineering. Three data points, one underlying message. The bar to generate top-quartile PE returns just went up materially, and a lot of the existing PE complex is not set up to clear it.

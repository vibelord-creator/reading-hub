One mechanism note before the deep dives, because it answers the question you should be asking: how can the market price rate hikes when growth is slowing? Isn't that backwards? The answer is that the Fed's two mandates are pulling in opposite directions. In a normal cycle, weak growth and low inflation arrive together, so cutting is easy. In a supply shock, weak growth and high inflation arrive together — that's the definition of stagflation — and the Fed has to choose which mandate to honor. Hiking into a slowdown is the choice that says inflation expectations matter more than this year's GDP, because once households start believing four percent is normal, wage demands and pricing behavior lock it in, and the eventual cure — a Volcker-style forced recession — costs far more than the slowdown you'd be tolerating now. That's why a hot PPI print can raise hike odds even as the economy decelerates: the market isn't saying the economy is strong, it's saying the Fed may decide credibility is the asset it can't afford to lose. Keep that distinction handy — it's the difference between reading the curve correctly and reading it backwards.

Now the deep dives.

Deep dive one: the stagflation test, and why the composition of this CPI print is the whole ballgame. The textbook distinction: demand-pull inflation comes from an overheating economy and responds to rate hikes; supply-shock inflation comes from something breaking — an oil chokepoint, say — and rate hikes can't reopen the Strait of Hormuz. The classic parallel is 1973, when OPEC's embargo took about seven percent of global supply offline and the Fed's stop-go response gave us a decade of stagflation. The 2026 version is stranger: the strait isn't embargoed, it's tolled. Iran is running Hormuz like a hostile toll road — over a million dollars per transit, capacity rationed. That's not a binary shock, it's a tax that flexes with geopolitics, which is why oil can be down twenty percent from peak and still be inflationary at 94 dollars. The orthodox central-banking doctrine says you look through supply shocks because they're self-correcting. The counterargument — and this is the hawks' case at next week's meeting — is that you only get to look through a shock if expectations stay anchored, and with headline at 4.2 and the BLS credibility question still open after the commissioner's firing, anchoring is exactly what's in doubt. Meanwhile the dovish evidence is real too: core goods are deflating, the tariff impulse is spent, and core at 2.9 is within shouting distance of target. So Warsh inherits the genuinely hard version of the problem: headline screaming supply shock, core whispering all-clear. McKinsey's scenario-planning discipline applies here — don't predict, bracket. Scenario one, the MOU holds, tolls ease, energy unwinds, and late-2026 looks fine. Scenario two, the ceasefire collapses, Brent retests its highs, and the hike the futures market is flirting with becomes real. The dot plot Wednesday tells you which scenario the committee is privately weighting.

Deep dive two: what the SpaceX listing actually prices, and what it does to everything around it. Start with the frame from McKinsey's power-curve research — economic profit concentrates brutally in a handful of firms at the top of the global distribution, and markets pay up for the perceived right to join that tail. A 1.77 trillion dollar valuation for SpaceX is a bet on exactly that: not on launch margins today, but on Starlink as a global utility, on launch monopoly economics, and on optionality — Mars, defense, whatever comes — that can't be modeled, only believed. The fixed-price mechanism is itself a tell. A normal IPO book-builds to discover the price; SpaceX announced 135 dollars and let demand come to it — that's a company telling the market what it's worth, not asking. Add thirty percent retail allocation and you have something closer to a public offering in the literal, populist sense — which is great marketing and also, the skeptics would note, a way to distribute paper at peak enthusiasm to the least price-sensitive buyers. The Aramco comparison is instructive: the previous record IPO listed at the top of an oil cycle, and the stock went roughly nowhere for years while the dividend did the work. SpaceX has no dividend; the return case is pure growth. Here's the so-what for your world: the first week of SPCX trading becomes the discount-rate-of-record for every AI-adjacent private name. If it holds 135 and runs, Anthropic's path from 965 billion to a trillion-plus is paved, and every late-stage AI round in the second half of 2026 marks up. If it breaks issue price in week one — at five percent thirty-year yields, with CPI at 4.2 — that's the signal that even transcendence has a discount rate. Watch it like an index.

Deep dive three, briefly: the pharma deal wave as a private-equity signal. A hundred and six billion dollars across 201 deals by early June is not opportunistic — it's structural. The patent cliff running through the late 2020s forces big pharma to buy growth, and "Strategy Beyond the Hockey Stick" gives the right language: big moves, made early, beat incrementalism — and pharma BD teams are making big moves at record pace. Notice what they're buying: Lilly's Ascidian deal is RNA exon editing — platform science, not products. When strategics pay up for platforms, the venture-backed biotech ecosystem gets its exit route back, and that flows directly into the healthcare PE/VC pitch you're building toward. The Hikma ruling is the asterisk: cheaper generic entry compresses the tail value of marketed drugs, which raises the premium on genuinely novel pipeline — reinforcing the platform-buying behavior. The whole loop is self-consistent, and it's the strongest fundamentals story in healthcare investing right now.

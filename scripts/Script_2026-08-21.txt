ANDREW: So. Mechanism workshop. How does a data center actually get financed, and where exactly does it break?

AVA: Start with what's being sold. A hyperscale data center is not really a building. It's a contract to deliver a certain number of megawatts of compute capacity to a tenant, for a term — call it ten to fifteen years. The building is the wrapper. The contract is the asset.

ANDREW: And the tenant is usually one of a very short list of names.

AVA: Which is the first thing to notice. This is single-tenant credit. If your data center is leased to one hyperscaler, you don't own a diversified real-estate asset. You own that company's credit, dressed up as property. Underwriters price it that way — the debt cost tracks the tenant's rating, not the neighborhood.

ANDREW: Now the power side.

AVA: The power side is where it gets interesting, and it's where the Journal piece is pointed. To build, you need firm power. You sign a long-term power purchase agreement — a PPA — that locks in supply at a set price, often with take-or-pay terms, meaning you pay whether or not you draw the electricity. That's the developer eating volume risk to give the utility revenue certainty, which is what makes the utility willing to build the interconnection in the first place.

ANDREW: So the developer is now short power at a fixed price and long a lease at a fixed price.

AVA: Correct, and that's a spread business. You're earning the difference between what the tenant pays you and what the power and the debt cost you. Spread businesses are fine — banks are spread businesses. The problem is leverage. These projects run at high loan-to-value because the contracted cash flows look bond-like. Seventy, seventy-five percent debt is not unusual. At that leverage, a modest move in either leg of the spread wipes out a large share of the equity.

ANDREW: Give me the arithmetic.

AVA: Simplified, but honest. Say the project earns a nine percent unlevered yield on cost, and you fund it seventy percent debt at six percent. Your equity return is roughly the nine, plus the seventy over thirty ratio times the three-point spread — call it sixteen percent. Now let debt cost rise to eight percent, because the long end sold off. Same asset, same tenant, same building. Equity return falls to about eleven and a half. You didn't lose a customer. You lost four and a half points of return to the bond market.

ANDREW: And that's the connection to yesterday.

AVA: That's the connection to yesterday. When the term premium rises — the extra yield investors demand for lending long — every long-duration asset reprices, and data centers are among the longest-duration assets anyone is building right now. The central bank can cut short rates all it likes. If the thirty-year is stubborn, the data-center pro forma is stubborn too.

ANDREW: Then what's the third failure mode? You said two prices, but the Journal's piece is about backers, not prices.

AVA: Residual value. At the end of a fifteen-year lease, what's the building worth? For an office tower you'd say a lot — you re-lease it. For a data center, the answer depends on whether the power density standard of twenty forty-one resembles the standard of twenty twenty-six. If chips get four times denser and cooling moves to something the building can't support, you own a warehouse with an excellent electrical service. That's the risk nobody underwrites honestly, because the residual assumption is the single easiest lever to flatter a model with.

ANDREW: And the Economist's claim that the backlash is fake?

AVA: I'd say the framing is right and the conclusion is complacent. Local opposition rarely stops a project outright — it delays it, and it extracts concessions. But delay is not free when your PPA has already started and your interest is capitalizing. The opposition doesn't need to win. It needs to cost you eighteen months. And on the other side, a16z's chart this week is a real point in the developers' favor — these sites are a genuine blue-collar employment story, construction and electrical trades, which is why the politics are messier than the Twitter version suggests.

ANDREW: So where does that leave the investor?

AVA: With a preference for the boring end of the stack. If you believe compute demand and doubt the financing terms, you'd rather own the power than the building — the utility, the turbine order book, the interconnection queue position. Those are the parts that stay scarce whether or not any individual data center pencils out. That's the granularity-of-growth point, actually: the returns aren't in the sector, they're in one layer of it.

If patient records genuinely move between systems, the incumbent EHR moat gets shallower and the whole care-navigation and payer-services category gets more contestable. Verma running Oracle Health means she's now on the incumbent side of a rule she helped write. That's worth noticing, and it's the kind of thing that makes a good question at a networking event rather than a good statement.

ANDREW: Right, let's do reverse mergers. Ambros is going public by merging into Werewolf, which is already listed. Why is that a signal?

AVA: Because of what it substitutes for. There are two ways into the public market. The front door is an IPO — you file, you roadshow, you price, and the process itself is a market test. The side door is to merge into an already-listed company that has a stock exchange listing and, usually, a disappointing pipeline and a pile of cash. You take the shell's listing; the shell's shareholders take a slice of your company.

ANDREW: And that trade only makes sense in specific conditions.

AVA: It makes sense when the front door is shut. Biotech issuance has been in a drought — that's been true through most of twenty twenty-five and into this year. When companies that need capital can't IPO, they go looking for shells. So the volume of reverse mergers is genuinely informative: it's a measure of how many companies want to be public divided by how many can be.

ANDREW: Historical parallel?

AVA: Two, and they point in opposite directions, which is why I'd be careful. The constructive one is two thousand nine and twenty ten — a wave of reverse mergers after the financial crisis that did in fact precede a real reopening of the biotech window. The cautionary one is twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-three, when the SPAC unwind left dozens of listed shells and the reverse-merger wave that followed was mostly companies dissolving into each other. Same mechanic, completely different meaning.

ANDREW: So how do you tell which one you're in?

AVA: Look at direction of travel. In a thaw, the private company is the one with the science and the shell is the one with the cash, and new money comes in alongside the merger — a concurrent private placement. In a wind-down, there's no new money; it's two balance sheets consolidating to extend runway. So the diagnostic question on Ambros is simple: did anyone write a fresh check as part of this deal? If yes, it's evidence of a thaw. If it's just the merger, it's cash-preservation with a listing attached.

ANDREW: That's a good question to have in your pocket.

AVA: It's better than the alternative, which is repeating a headline about the biotech window reopening and being asked why.

ANDREW: AI. Three items and a long read.

AVA: Cerebras announced the CS-4 — wafer-scale, claiming roughly ten times the efficiency of the CS-3. We flagged this yesterday too, and my caution is unchanged: efficiency claims from specialty silicon are measured on workloads the vendor selected. The strategic question isn't whether the chip is fast. It's whether anything other than Nvidia can accumulate a software ecosystem, because that's the actual moat and it's the thing every challenger has failed at for a decade.

ANDREW: Second, from The Batch: Anthropic is watermarking outputs, and Qwen's open-weight models are surging.

AVA: Those belong together. The gap between the best closed model and the best open-weight model keeps compressing, and that compression is the single most important variable in enterprise build-versus-buy. If open weights are ninety percent as good at a fraction of the cost, the frontier labs' pricing power has to come from somewhere other than raw capability — reliability, indemnification, compliance, deployment. Watermarking is a small piece of that same move: it's provenance as a product feature.

ANDREW: Third, Duolingo as an AI content engine — from Business Analytics.

AVA: A genuinely good case study, and it maps onto marketing coursework cleanly. Duolingo used generative models to collapse the marginal cost of producing course content. What that does is change the shape of the catalog: when content costs almost nothing to make, you can serve the long tail of small languages that could never justify a human curriculum team. The customer-lifetime-value implication is that acquisition cost in a niche stops being the binding constraint, because the content to serve that niche is nearly free. That's an argument you can use in any content business, not just this one.

ANDREW: And the Economist's long read on whether AI could be conscious.

AVA: Flag it for the backlog rather than skimming. The reason to read it isn't the philosophy. It's that this is how a serious generalist publication is framing the question for non-technical executives — and that framing is what the people across the table from you will have read.

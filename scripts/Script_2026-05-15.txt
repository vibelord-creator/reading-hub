Deep dive one. The 1973 to 1974 stagflation playbook is the closest historical parallel to today's tape, and you should be ready to use the analog in any IC discussion this quarter. In October 1973, OPEC's oil embargo lifted crude from three dollars to twelve dollars per barrel in a matter of months. Arthur Burns chaired the Fed. He misread the shock as transitory, cut rates into it, and CPI ran double-digits through 1980. Paul Volcker then had to hike Fed Funds to twenty percent to break the inflation cycle. The Volcker pivot took two recessions and four years of nine-percent-plus unemployment. The cost of Burns being wrong was a decade of stagflation. The cost of Volcker being right was the early 1980s recession.

Now translate to today. The Iran shock is doing in five months what the 1973 OPEC embargo did in eighteen months. Warsh inherits the same temptation Burns faced, with the same trap. The Burns mistake was that he genuinely believed the shock would prove transitory, and he was philosophically aligned with the Nixon White House on accommodative monetary policy. Warsh has now publicly made the AI-productivity case for cuts, which is structurally the same argument Burns made about supply-side recovery. The market's eighty-percent relative repricing in thirty days reflects a tape that has begun pricing in the non-Burns outcome, namely that Warsh will be forced into Volcker-style credibility instead.
